## Summary

Hama is built around a bend of the Orontes River, best known for its norias — 17 surviving wooden waterwheels, the largest nearly 21 metres across, that once lifted river water into stone aqueducts to irrigate the town and its gardens. Danish archaeologists documented roughly 20 wheels in the 1930s; by 2009 all had gone out of use as working irrigation infrastructure, and today they are conserved as the city's defining sight and sound, though restoration is complicated by a shortage of craftspeople skilled in traditional noria carpentry.

Downtown, the 12th-century Al-Nuri Mosque and the 1742 Ottoman-era Azm Palace (now the regional archaeology museum) were both damaged by shelling in 1982 and later restored — the palace after 2005, with work by the German Archaeological Institute. About 45 minutes west of Hama, the hilltop fortress of Masyaf sits on Byzantine foundations; in 1141 it passed to the Nizari Ismaili community, a branch of Shia Islam, and became their Syrian seat in the later 12th century under Rashid al-Din Sinan. It has been under restoration since 2000 with support from the Aga Khan Trust for Culture.

Northwest of Hama, the Seleucid city of Apamea (founded around 300 BC by Seleucus I Nicator) preserves one of the longest colonnaded streets of the ancient world — close to 1,850 metres lined with over 1,000 columns — running past the remains of a Roman theatre that seated more than 20,000. Apamea suffered severe looting after 2011: 2017 satellite imagery documented hundreds of illegal excavation pits, and a later drone survey by Syria's antiquities authority (DGAM) recorded a still higher figure, in the thousands. UNESCO reporting from 2017-2018 also noted that on-the-ground access to the site was restricted by landmines; whether that restriction still applies in 2026 was not confirmed in this research. Apamea is on UNESCO's Tentative List (since 1999) but has never been fully inscribed; in May 2026 it was one of nine Syrian sites added to the heritage lists of ICESCO (the Islamic World Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization — some 2026 Syrian press still uses its pre-2020 name, ISESCO), the first such Syrian listings in 14 years.

As of September 2026, the destination sits inside Syria's uneven reopening. Commercial flights to Damascus have resumed with multiple carriers, and land crossings function, but access has been interrupted before and can be again: Damascus airport suspended commercial operations for roughly six weeks (28 February-8 April 2026) during an Iran-Israel escalation, and Damascus itself saw two bombings in July 2026. The US State Department, UK FCDO and Australian government continue to rate the whole country at their highest advisory level (US: Level 4, "Do Not Travel"). This is not a blanket judgment on the country — access and conditions vary significantly by region and shift quickly — but it means checking the current advisory and a reputable local operator before travel, not relying on this page alone.

## Highlights

### The Norias of Hama — نواعير حماة
Seventeen wooden waterwheels along the Orontes, the largest — Noria al-Muhammadiya — nearly 21 metres in diameter and dated 1361 CE. Danish archaeologists recorded about 20 wheels in the 1930s; all had stopped functioning as working irrigation infrastructure by 2009.
**Condition:** Conserved; one wheel was deliberately burned in 2014 during the war and others lost timber to pillaging, but the majority survive and are maintained as a heritage attraction — restoration work is constrained today by a shortage of craftspeople trained in traditional noria carpentry.

### Al-Nuri Mosque — الجامع النوري
A living Ayyubid-era mosque founded by Nur al-Din Zangi in 1163-64, historically noted for a carved wooden minbar from the same period, now preserved in Hama's museum. It is a place of active worship in the city centre, not a historical curiosity.
**Condition:** Damaged by shelling in 1982, subsequently restored. Current 2026 opening status was not independently confirmed in this research — check locally.

### Azm Palace, Hama — قصر العظم بحماة
An Ottoman governor's residence built in 1742 by As'ad Pasha al-Azm, near the citadel mound on the Orontes. Converted to Hama's regional archaeology museum in 1956, it displays finds from across the province, including Apamea.
**Condition:** Damaged in the 1982 bombardment of Hama, restored after 2005 (work associated with the German Archaeological Institute). Functioned as a museum in pre-2026 sources; current 2026 opening status not independently confirmed.

### Apamea (Afamia) — the Great Colonnade — أفاميا
A Seleucid city founded around 300 BC, later a major Roman-Byzantine centre. Its colonnaded main street runs close to 1,850 metres, lined with more than 1,000 columns; a large Roman theatre (capacity over 20,000) also survives. Architectural details beyond this — such as specific column-carving styles or later imperial-era additions — were not independently confirmed and are omitted here rather than guessed at.
**Condition:** The standing colonnade and theatre structures are intact, but the site suffered extensive looting from 2011 onward — 2017 satellite imagery recorded hundreds of illegal excavation pits, and a subsequent DGAM drone survey put the number in the thousands. UNESCO reporting from 2017-2018 described site access as restricted by landmines; current (2026) clearance status is unconfirmed. On Syria's UNESCO Tentative List since 1999, not fully inscribed; added to ICESCO's heritage lists in May 2026.

### Masyaf Castle — قلعة مصياف
A hilltop fortress on Byzantine foundations. It passed to the Nizari Ismaili community — a branch of Shia Islam with a continuing presence in Syria today, including around Masyaf and Salamiyah — in 1141, and became their Syrian seat under Rashid al-Din Sinan in the later 12th century, later held by Mamluks and Ottomans.
**Condition:** Under restoration since 2000 with support from the Aga Khan Trust for Culture. Current civil-war-era condition and visitor access could not be independently confirmed in this research and should be checked locally before a visit.

## UNESCO status
Not inscribed. Apamea (Afamia) is on Syria's UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List (submitted 1999, criterion iv). Hama's norias, Al-Nuri Mosque, Azm Palace and Masyaf Castle are not on the Tentative List. None of Syria's six inscribed World Heritage Sites (Ancient City of Damascus; Site of Palmyra; Ancient City of Bosra; Ancient City of Aleppo; Crac des Chevaliers and Qal'at Salah El-Din; Ancient Villages of Northern Syria) are in Hama Governorate. All six have been on the UNESCO List of World Heritage in Danger since June 2013.

## Conservation note
Damage here has two distinct sources and periods and should not be conflated. Al-Nuri Mosque and the Azm Palace were both damaged by shelling in 1982 and were restored over the following decades. Separately, Apamea suffered extensive looting after the 2011 civil war began: 2017 satellite imagery documented hundreds of illegal excavation pits, and a later drone survey by Syria's Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums recorded a still higher figure, in the thousands — the two figures come from different surveys using different methods and shouldn't be read as a contradiction. The colonnade and theatre structures themselves remain standing; the looting damaged buried archaeological strata around and beneath them. UNESCO state-of-conservation reporting from 2017-2018 described physical access to the site as limited by landmines; this research found no source confirming or updating that status for 2026. In May 2026, Syria's antiquities authority announced Apamea's inclusion on ICESCO's heritage lists, the first such listing for Syrian sites in 14 years — officials frame it as a step toward strengthened preservation, though no detailed restoration timeline was found. The norias have fared comparatively well: most survived the war, with one confirmed case of deliberate arson (2014) and some timber loss, and are maintained today as a heritage attraction rather than functioning infrastructure, though a shortage of traditionally trained noria craftspeople constrains repair work.

## Access note
Hama city sits on the main Damascus-Aleppo highway and is reachable by road from both; Apamea is roughly 45 minutes to an hour's drive northwest of Hama, and Masyaf roughly 45 minutes to the west. Damascus International Airport has multiple carriers operating scheduled service in 2026 (Qatar Airways resumed daily flights from 1 May 2026; Etihad relaunched service in June 2026; other Gulf and Turkish carriers also serve the route), and land border crossings are functioning. Access has been interrupted before, though: Damascus airport suspended commercial operations for roughly six weeks, from 28 February to 8 April 2026, during a period of Iran-Israel regional escalation and Syrian airspace closures, before resuming after a ceasefire. Damascus also saw two bombings in July 2026 — a cafe bombing on 2 July (nine dead) and twin IEDs near the Ministry of Tourism on 7 July, during a state visit, with an ISIS-linked cell arrested by Syrian authorities on 9 July — plus continuing risk from Iran-related regional strikes since 8 July. As of September 2026 the US State Department, UK FCDO and Australian government continue to rate the whole country at their highest advisory level (US: Level 4, "Do Not Travel"), citing armed conflict, terrorism and arbitrary detention risk among others. Separately, UNESCO reporting from 2017-2018 noted landmine-restricted access at Apamea specifically; current clearance status is unconfirmed. This is not a blanket "safe" or "dangerous" verdict — access and conditions vary by area and shift quickly. Check the current advisory and travel with a reputable local operator rather than relying on this page alone.

## Local flavour
Hama is known nationally for its riverside qahwas (coffeehouses) built at the base of the norias, where the wheels' deep groan is part of the evening atmosphere. The city is also associated with halawet el-jibn and other syrup-and-cheese pastries sold around the old town — a well-established culinary association, though not independently verified against a fetched source in this research pass.

## Best season
Spring (March-May) and autumn (September-November), for mild temperatures walking the colonnade at Apamea and the hillside approach to Masyaf; summers in the Orontes valley are hot.
