## One-liner

A black-basalt Roman theatre enclosed inside a medieval citadel, in a living Daraa town now cautiously reopening to visitors.

## Summary

Bosra began as a Nabataean city and, after Trajan's annexation in 106 CE, became capital of the Roman province of Arabia as Nova Trajana Bostra — a major waypoint on routes between Damascus and the Hijaz. Its centerpiece is a 2nd-century Roman theatre built of local black basalt, widely cited as one of the best-preserved Roman theatres in the empire; sources give its seating capacity as somewhere between roughly 15,000 and 17,000 (accounts differ on whether standing areas are counted). In the Ayyubid period (12th–13th century) the theatre was enclosed within a multi-towered citadel — search-surfaced sources give eight or nine towers, though this was not confirmed against a directly fetched primary source, so treat the exact count as unresolved rather than the "seven" sometimes repeated online — built to guard the road to Damascus, which incidentally shielded the theatre's interior for centuries.

Bosra also carries deep early-Islamic heritage. The al-Omari Mosque was founded during the 7th-century conquest under Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab and completed in 721 CE under the Umayyad Caliph Yazid II; it is counted among the oldest surviving mosques in Islamic history and remains an active place of worship. The Jami' al-Mabrak, at the northeast edge of the old town, is a living shrine-mosque tied to local tradition about the Prophet Muhammad's youthful journey through Bosra; medieval Arab chroniclers did not all record the specific camel-kneeling detail, though the broader tradition of his stay in the town is well attested. The al-Khidr Mosque, also black-basalt, was built in 1134 on the orders of Kumushtakin, the Burid-period governor of Bosra (this is Burid Damascus — the Ayyubids did not take Damascus until 1174).

Bosra was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980 and placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013 as Daraa Governorate — where the 2011 uprising began — became an active front. The town changed hands during the war (opposition control from March 2015, government recapture in July 2018) and sustained documented damage, including to the al-Omari Mosque's roof in 2014 and to the theatre's western courtyard and parts of the citadel in December 2015. Restoration has advanced since the area was secured, and by September 2026 the Roman Theatre and Abu al-Fida School restorations were reported complete, with citadel work ongoing and visitor numbers rising — though the site remains on the Danger List.

## Highlights

### Roman Theatre of Bosra — المسرح الروماني في بصرى
2nd-century, 102-metre black basalt theatre, seating estimated at 15,000–17,000 depending on the source. Sources describe it as one of the best-preserved Roman theatres anywhere, and Wikipedia specifically notes it is the only theatre of its type with the upper gallery's covered portico fully preserved — well preserved in part because it was later fully enclosed inside an Ayyubid fortress rather than left exposed or scavenged for stone.
**Condition:** Restored 1947–1970; reported as fully restored again in September 2026 reporting; on the Danger List since 2013, though the theatre structure itself is consistently described as the best-preserved part of the site.

### Ayyubid Citadel enclosing the theatre — القلعة الأيوبية
A 12th–13th century fortress with, per search-surfaced (not directly fetched) sources, eight or nine towers — the tower count is not confirmed against a primary source and should be verified before publication — built directly around the Roman theatre to guard the Damascus road, a rare case of a citadel built over a still-legible ancient theatre.
**Condition:** Sustained war-related damage documented by UNESCO in December 2015; restoration was reported "underway" as of September 2026, not yet complete.

### Al-Omari Mosque (Mosque of Omar) — المسجد العمري
Founded during the 7th-century conquest under Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab and completed in 721 CE under Caliph Yazid II; one of the earliest surviving mosques in Islamic history, built partly with reused Roman material and an early Umayyad square minaret. It remains an active mosque, not a ruin.
**Condition:** A 2014 shell strike opened the roof and damaged the upper level; the interior and facade have since been reconstructed. No 2025–26 reopening date found in sources consulted.

### Jami' al-Mabrak (Mabrak Mosque/shrine) — جامع مبرك الناقة
A living shrine-mosque at the northeast edge of old Bosra, tied by local tradition to the Prophet Muhammad's youth visit to the town. Its prayer niche likely predates Islam; the site saw Abbasid-era expansion (8th–10th century) and major reorganization in the 12th–13th century, when Kumushtakin added an adjoining madrasa (1136). Medieval Arab writers who documented the tradition of the Prophet Muhammad's stay in Bosra did not all record the specific camel-kneeling detail — worth presenting as living local tradition rather than settled history.
**Condition:** Standing; detailed post-2011 condition not found in sources consulted.

### Al-Khidr Mosque — مسجد الخضر
A black-basalt mosque built in 1134 (528 AH) on the orders of Kumushtakin, the Burid-period governor of Bosra, incorporating reused Nabataean and Greco-Roman stonework. Its square minaret stands 17 metres tall (not ~12 m as sometimes stated) and is believed to date from the 13th century, modeled on the Great Mosque's minaret.
**Condition:** Standing; detailed post-2011 condition not found in sources consulted.

## UNESCO status

Inscribed World Heritage Site since 1980 under criteria (i), (iii) and (vi). Placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013 (decision 37 COM 7B.57, 37th session), together with Syria's other five World Heritage properties, due to conflict-related risk. As of the most current dated reporting found (2025–26 secondary sources; UNESCO's own pages returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch today), corrective measures remain "in progress" and Bosra has not been reported as removed from the Danger List.

## Conservation note

Bosra sits in Daraa Governorate, where the 2011 uprising began; the town itself was a battleground, held by opposition forces from 25 March 2015 until the Syrian Arab Army recaptured it on 2 July 2018. UNESCO's Director-General documented specific damage in a December 2015 statement — to the theatre's western courtyard and to parts of the Ayyubid citadel — without attributing responsibility to a specific party. Separately, the al-Omari Mosque took a shell hit that opened its roof and destroyed its upper level in 2014; reconstruction of the interior and facade has since been carried out. Restoration broadly resumed after the July 2018 government recapture (the original brief's "secured around 2019" figure was not independently confirmed in this research and is not repeated here). By September 2026, Syria's Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums, working with international partners, reported the Roman Theatre and the Abu al-Fida School restorations complete, citadel restoration ongoing, and a new visitor amenity (Trajana Park) opened — alongside a real rise in visitor numbers (10,831 visitors in the second half of 2026 to date, up from 4,530 in the same period of 2025, per Syria's state news agency). The site remains on the Danger List as of the most recent reporting found; no source consulted reports delisting.

## Access note

Bosra is approximately 140 km south of Damascus and roughly 40 km from Daraa city — this distance is now confirmed by a dated 14 September 2026 report, not just older or unverified sources. As of that same reporting, the town has reopened to tourism on a small scale: more than 50 foreign tour groups had visited since the start of 2026, domestic tourism (school and family trips) picked up from April 2026, and dedicated tourist police patrols were deployed. Syria's government changed in December 2024, and the situation continues to evolve; road conditions, checkpoint practices, and permit requirements have shifted repeatedly since 2011. Anyone publishing precise, actionable travel logistics (opening hours, ticketing, current permit rules) should still verify directly with Syria's Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums and current government travel advisories rather than relying on this brief alone — access is reopening, not settled.

## Local flavour

Bosra's black basalt is the town's visual signature — the same volcanic stone used for the Roman theatre, the citadel, and the al-Khidr Mosque gives the whole site a distinct dark-grey monumentality unlike the limestone ruins elsewhere in Syria, reflecting the Hauran plateau's volcanic geology.

## Best season

Not independently verified in this research pass; the Hauran plateau has a semi-arid climate with cold, sometimes wet winters and hot, dry summers, which typically makes spring and autumn the more comfortable visiting months at ruins sites in this region generally — flagged here as a reasonable regional inference, not a site-specific verified claim.

## Corrections made (vs. the draft card)

1. **Citadel tower count — corrected.** The draft said "seven-towered citadel" twice. No source found in this research supports seven; multiple sources (Archnet, Ancient Origins, general architectural descriptions) cite either eight or nine towers, with nine somewhat more common. Changed to "eight or nine towers (sources vary)."
2. **Theatre seating capacity — softened from a flat number to a range.** The draft stated "roughly 17,000" as settled fact. Sources genuinely disagree (commonly 15,000 or 17,000; some cite much lower seated-only figures before adding standing capacity). Changed to "estimated 15,000–17,000."
3. **Al-Khidr Mosque minaret height — corrected.** The draft said "roughly 12 metres." The French Ministry of Culture's archaeological heritage page (archeologie.culture.gouv.fr) gives 17 metres. Corrected to 17 m and flagged the discrepancy with lower-quality sources that say 12 m.
4. **Al-Omari Mosque completion — resolved, not left as an open riskyClaim.** The draft flagged uncertainty between "Caliph Yazid II" and "Caliph Omar II." Wikipedia's dedicated article is unambiguous: founded 636 CE under Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab, completed 721 CE under Caliph Yazid II. No "Umar II" attribution was found in any source consulted. Resolved in favor of Umar (founder) + Yazid II (completed).
5. **Mabrak Mosque description — reframed for accuracy and tone.** The draft's "resting place on an old caravan route" was vague. The actual, better-sourced tradition is specifically about the Prophet Muhammad's youth visit to Bosra (linked to the Bahira-the-monk episode in the sira tradition), with the camel-kneeling detail specifically disputed by some medieval Arab chroniclers even where they affirm the broader visit. Rewritten to present this as living local/devotional tradition — consistent with treating a place of worship as heritage and faith, not a curiosity — rather than asserting the legend as settled fact.
6. **Danger List inscription year — confirmed, not left ambiguous.** The draft hedged between 2012 and 2013. The 2013 date is corroborated by the WHC Committee's own decision record (37 COM 7B.57, 37th session, 2013) and by multiple independent secondary sources; treated as reliable. (A fetch of the "Ancient City of Bosra" Wikipedia article during this research returned "2017" for the Danger List year — this conflicts with the WHC decision record and every other source consulted and was not used; its origin, whether a Wikipedia article error or a fetch-summarization artifact, was not determined.)
7. **Access and conservation status — upgraded from "unverified as of 2026" to sourced.** The draft could not find any post-June-2024 reporting and explicitly flagged current accessibility as unverified. This research found a dated 14 September 2026 Syrian state-media report (SANA) with current visitor numbers, restoration status by site, and the 140 km distance figure. The card's caution about verifying logistics before publishing is kept, but the blanket "no current status found" framing is no longer accurate and has been updated.
8. **UNESCO source pages — re-confirmed as inaccessible, not newly broken.** Direct fetches of whc.unesco.org/en/soc/1951/, /en/list/22/, and a July 2026 WHC document all returned HTTP 403 again today. The underlying facts (1980 inscription, criteria i/iii/vi, 2013 Danger List year) were independently corroborated through the official WHC decision record surfaced via search and multiple secondary sources, but a direct, authoritative full-page read of UNESCO's own site was not achieved and should be attempted again before publication if possible.
9. **Tone check — no changes needed structurally.** The draft already avoided blanket "safe"/"dangerous" language about Syria, described war damage factually without romanticizing it, and did not name a perpetrator where sources didn't. The corrected version keeps this discipline and strengthens the mosques' framing as living, active places of worship (e.g., noting al-Omari remains an active mosque) rather than tourist curiosities. Arsu (the pre-Islamic Palmyrene deity) is not referenced anywhere in this card — no issue to address.
10. **Population — unchanged.** No verified post-2011 figure was found in this research; the 2004 census figure is retained with the same caveat as the original draft.
11. **Kumushtakin's title — corrected (error introduced and caught during this review).** An earlier draft of this corrected file mislabeled Kumushtakin as an "Ayyubid-era governor's officer." Per the directly fetched French Ministry of Culture source, he was an officer of the Burid Atabeg Tughtakin and then governor of Bosra himself, in 1134 — Burid Damascus, not Ayyubid (the Ayyubids did not take Damascus until 1174). Fixed throughout; noted here because the original card did not make this mistake — it was introduced during fact-checking and corrected before publication.
