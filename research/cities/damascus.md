# Damascus (دمشق) — Corrected Fact Sheet

**Slug:** damascus
**Region:** Damascus Governorate, southwestern Syria — on the Barada River at the edge of the Ghouta oasis, roughly 40 km from the Lebanese border via the Jdeidat Yabous crossing (M2 highway; the Masnaa crossing on the Lebanese side is about 30-40 minutes' drive further), and about 85 km inland from Beirut
**One-liner:** Layers of Aramean, Roman, Umayyad and Ottoman Damascus, still lived-in behind the Old City walls

---

## Summary

Damascus is one of a small handful of cities — along with Aleppo, Byblos and Jericho — with a genuine but contested claim to continuous habitation since deep antiquity. The claim is disputed among archaeologists and should never be stated as settled fact. Carbon-14 dating at nearby Tell Ramad points to occupation from around 6300 BC, though the walled city itself is conventionally dated to the 3rd millennium BC. UNESCO inscribed the Ancient City of Damascus as a World Heritage Site in 1979 (criteria i, ii, iii, iv, vi), citing roughly 125 monuments spanning Aramean, Roman, Byzantine, Umayyad, Ayyubid, Mamluk and Ottoman periods. The Old City is still ringed by about 4.5 km of walls pierced by eight gates, seven of them tracing back to a Roman circuit originally dedicated to seven planetary deities.

At its heart stands the Umayyad Mosque, built 705-715 CE on a site that held an Aramean sanctuary to Hadad, a Roman temple to Jupiter, then a Byzantine church — its shrine is venerated by both Muslims and Christians as holding relics associated with John the Baptist. Adjoining it is the 1196 mausoleum of Saladin. Souq al-Hamidiyya leads to the mosque past the Roman-era Straight Street (Via Recta), named in the Book of Acts, while the Midhat Pasha souq and the 18th-century Azm Palace showcase Damascene courtyard architecture. East of the mosque, Bab Touma remains the historic Christian quarter.

Following the fall of the Assad government in December 2024, tourism to Syria is reopening in stages, not returning to normal all at once. Treat any claim about site access or visitor conditions as current only as of when it was checked.

## Highlights

### Umayyad Mosque (Great Mosque of Damascus) — الجامع الأموي
Built 705-715 CE for Caliph al-Walid I on a site that held an Aramean sanctuary, a Roman temple to Jupiter, and a Byzantine church dedicated to John the Baptist. A living, active place of worship for Muslims, with a shrine also venerated by Christians.
**Condition:** Open and active; a functioning mosque and major visitor site.

### Souq al-Hamidiyya — سوق الحميدية
A roughly 600-metre, 15-metre-wide covered market leading straight to the Umayyad Mosque's entrance. Its metal roof still bears holes attributed to French artillery fire during the 1925 Great Syrian Revolt.
**Condition:** Open and operating; reported as busy in 2025-2026 travel coverage.

### Straight Street (Via Recta) and Midhat Pasha Souq — الشارع المستقيم / سوق مدحت باشا
A Roman colonnaded thoroughfare, roughly 1,570 m long and 26 m wide in antiquity, named in the Book of Acts (9:11) as the street associated with Paul's stay in Damascus. Today its western stretch is the Midhat Pasha souq; scattered Roman-era colonnade remains are visible.
**Condition:** Open, walkable.

### Azm Palace — قصر العظم
An 18th-century Damascene courtyard mansion built 1749-1750 (sources differ by a year) for Ottoman governor As'ad Pasha al-Azm, now the Museum of Arts and Popular Traditions.
**Condition:** Functioning museum; verify current opening hours before visiting.

### Mausoleum of Saladin — ضريح صلاح الدين الأيوبي
Built in 1196, three years after Saladin's death, beside the Umayyad Mosque. Holds his original wooden sarcophagus. A marble sarcophagus was added in the late 19th century — most sources credit Ottoman Sultan Abdulhamid II as commissioner, with a restoration and visit by German Kaiser Wilhelm II in 1898, though sourcing on exactly who gave what is inconsistent (see Corrections).
**Condition:** Intact, restored under Ottoman rule (1898); open to visitors.

### Bab Touma (Christian Quarter) — باب توما
The historic Christian district in the Old City's northeast corner, home to a below-ground chapel traditionally associated with Ananias in the biblical account of Paul's baptism, and dense Damascene courtyard housing.
**Condition:** Inhabited residential quarter, open to visitors.

### Mount Qasioun — جبل قاسيون
A 1,151 m ridge overlooking the city, long valued for its views and cafés. Public access was restricted on security and military grounds through the civil war and was restored following the December 2024 change of government.
**Condition:** Reopening gradually; some viewpoints and the former restaurant strip are only recently accessible — confirm current access before visiting.

## UNESCO Status

Inscribed 1979 as the Ancient City of Damascus (criteria i, ii, iii, iv, vi). Placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger in June 2013 along with the five other Syrian World Heritage sites (Palmyra, Bosra, Aleppo, Crac des Chevaliers/Qal'at Salah El-Din, and the Ancient Villages of Northern Syria), in response to risks from the civil war. Per World Heritage Committee decision 47 COM 7A.50 (2025 session), the Ancient City of Damascus was retained on the Danger List, with the decision citing factors including war damage, housing pressure, and management gaps; 2023 reporting noted emergency repair of the city wall between Bab Touma and Bab al-Salam using World Heritage Fund support. This is confirmed via search snippets of the decision and related UNESCO news pages — whc.unesco.org itself returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch attempts in this research session, so treat the exact current wording of the decision as unconfirmed against the primary document.

## Conservation Note

Damascus's Old City was comparatively spared the direct, large-scale destruction seen in Aleppo's Old City or at Palmyra — it was not a frontline battle zone in the same way. That said, years of war, sanctions, population strain and underinvestment left the site on UNESCO's Danger List, and heritage organizations (e.g., ASOR's Cultural Heritage Initiatives) have monitored risk across Syrian sites since 2014. Restoration and reopening are active and uneven as of 2026: the Citadel of Damascus, closed to the public through the war (and only partly open even before 2011), reopened in the spring of 2026 after conservation work, with a new "Damascus Heritage House" cultural space inaugurated there in early 2026; Mount Qasioun's viewpoints are reopening gradually rather than fully restored. Any specific claim about a site's current physical condition should be reverified close to a visit date.

## Access Note

Damascus International Airport has seen resumed service since the change of government in January 2025, including Qatar Airways (resumed 7 January 2025, its first Damascus service in about 13 years), Royal Jordanian (resumed 31 January 2025) and Turkish Airlines, among a growing list of carriers. Visa procedures are reported by some travel-industry outlets to have eased for a number of passport holders since early 2025, but this research could not independently verify current visa-on-arrival policy against an official Syrian government source — confirm directly before travel. The U.S. State Department has maintained a Level 4 "Do Not Travel" advisory for Syria since the advisory system began in 2018, citing terrorism, civil unrest, kidnapping, crime and armed conflict; other governments' advisories differ and should be checked directly. A Ministry of Tourism/Ministry of Interior Tourism Police unit began training in 2025: a first batch graduated from the Damascus police academy in June 2025 and 60 officers were deployed to the Damascus International Fair in August 2025; a later graduating cohort of roughly 518 (reported from a Tartous training course) is a training/graduation figure, not necessarily all currently deployed on Damascus streets. Old City markets and the Umayyad Mosque are reported open and busy in 2025-2026 coverage; the Citadel and some Mount Qasioun viewpoints are only recently or partially reopened. Confirm hours, security conditions and any permit requirements immediately before a visit rather than relying on any date-stamped source, including this one.

## Best Season

Spring (March-May) and autumn (September-November), when Damascus's semi-arid climate is mild; summers are hot and dry, winters cool and occasionally wet.

## Local Flavour

Damascene courtyard houses reflect a distinct residential craft tradition, and several have been restored as boutique hotels in the Old City — this research could not verify specific hotel names or operating status as of September 2026 and none is named here for that reason. The city is historically associated with Damask textiles, inlaid woodwork (Damascene marquetry), and Damask rose products, the rose itself well documented as cultivated in the nearby Qalamoun region.
