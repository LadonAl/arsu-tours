## One-liner

Byzantine farming villages, abandoned a millennium ago, across Idlib's limestone hills.

## Summary

The Dead Cities — officially the Ancient Villages of Northern Syria — are the ruins of roughly 40 rural settlements, grouped into eight archaeological parks, scattered across the limestone hills of Idlib and western Aleppo governorates. Built mostly between the 1st and 7th centuries CE and abandoned by the 10th, they preserve an unusually complete record of provincial life at the end of antiquity: stone houses, oil and wine presses, bathhouses, tombs, and some of the earliest surviving church architecture in the region, built as the area shifted from Roman paganism to Byzantine Christianity. UNESCO inscribed the site as a World Heritage property in 2011 and, two years into the Syrian civil war, added it to the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013, citing the threat the conflict posed to the country's cultural heritage broadly. Since then, individual sites have suffered specific, documented harm — an airstrike damaged the martyrium of Saint Simeon Stylites in 2016, shelling and reuse by displaced families altered parts of Serjilla, and stones from a church at al-Bara were dismantled for modern construction between 2010 and 2021. Conservation work, including at Qalb Lozeh's basilica, resumed in 2022. Idlib has only recently become reachable for visitors following the change of government in late 2024.

## Highlights

### 1. Serjilla (سرجيلا)
One of the best-preserved Dead Cities, founded around 473 CE in the Jebel Riha hills near al-Bara. Its ruins include a bathhouse, an olive press, multi-storey villas, and tombs that once served a prosperous farming community.
**Condition:** Largely intact; a rehabilitated guesthouse in the western part of the site lost its roof in a 2019 airstrike attributed to Russian forces, and displaced families altered some structures earlier in the war.

### 2. Al-Bara (البارة)
The most extensive of the Dead Cities' ruin fields, founded in the 4th century and abandoned after a 12th-century earthquake. At least five churches, three monasteries, and two pyramid-shaped tombs survive scattered among olive groves.
**Condition:** Ruins are dispersed and partly overgrown; a structure known as the Citadel Church was progressively dismantled for building stone between roughly 2010 and 2021 according to satellite-based monitoring, and some tombs have been looted.

### 3. Qalb Lozeh basilica (قلب لوزة)
A basilica church built in the 460s CE, whose carved facade and twin-tower entrance made it one of the most admired buildings of the late-antique Levant. Some architectural historians (following early assessments by Gertrude Bell) have compared its facade design to later European church architecture.
**Condition:** The facade and much of the structure still stand; the building suffered neglect, informal use as livestock shelter, and graffiti after caretakers left in 2011. Conservation work, including debris removal and floor repairs, resumed in 2022.

### 4. Church of Saint Simeon Stylites — Qalaat Samaan (قلعة سمعان)
A vast martyrium built between roughly 473 and 490 CE around the base of the pillar where the ascetic monk Simeon Stylites lived for decades; at the time of construction it was among the largest churches in the world.
**Condition:** Part of the pillar remnant and surrounding ruins were damaged in a May 2016 airstrike attributed to the Russian air force. The site remains a ruin, not a functioning church.

### 5. Jerada (جرادة)
A hillside settlement above the old Aleppo–Hama road, with the remains of a 5th-century church, a six-storey watchtower, and large houses that once belonged to a prosperous farming elite.
**Condition:** Partly reoccupied — unlike most Dead Cities, some of the old houses are used by local villagers as barns and storage rather than standing empty.

### 6. Mushabbak basilica
A 5th-century basilica on a hilltop west of Aleppo, on the old route to Saint Simeon's shrine, with its columned nave and wall openings still standing nearly complete.
**Condition:** Structurally close to intact apart from its original roof and window glazing; regarded as one of the more architecturally complete Dead Cities churches.

## UNESCO status

Inscribed 2011 as "Ancient Villages of Northern Syria" under criteria (iii), (iv) and (v). Added to the List of World Heritage in Danger on 20 June 2013, alongside Syria's five other World Heritage properties, in response to the threat the civil war posed to the country's cultural heritage. It remains on the Danger List as of the most recent published UNESCO State of Conservation reporting found in this research.

## Conservation note

War-related damage is documented at specific sites rather than uniformly across the property: an airstrike attributed to Russian forces damaged the remaining pillar and ruins of the Church of Saint Simeon Stylites in May 2016; a similarly attributed strike hit the western part of Serjilla in May 2019, destroying the roof of a restored guesthouse; and satellite-based monitoring by a Syrian heritage-documentation initiative recorded the progressive dismantling of al-Bara's Citadel Church for building stone between roughly 2010 and 2021. Displaced families sheltering at several sites during the war altered some structures, and looting for the illicit antiquities trade has been reported at tombs, including at al-Bara. Against that, there is also a documented recovery step: conservation work at the Qalb Lozeh basilica resumed in 2022. UNESCO's own published damage assessments for the property predate Syria's change of government in December 2024 and describe the site as inaccessible for full survey; no post-2024 UNESCO mission report specific to the Dead Cities was located in this research, so the current, on-the-ground physical condition of the wider property is not independently confirmed here.

## Access note

Access to Syria is governed by a country-wide advisory, not a site-specific one: as of this research, the UK FCDO "advises against all travel to Syria due to unpredictable security conditions and the threat of terrorist attacks," with no separate carve-out found for Idlib. Idlib Governorate, where most of the Dead Cities sit, only became reachable to general travellers after the change of government in December 2024 — for most of the preceding decade it was outside government control. Multiple 2025–2026 travel accounts describe the wider region as newly open but note ordinary features of travel there: road checkpoints, conservative local dress norms (headscarves expected for women), and patchy mobile network coverage. No dedicated permit system or official visitor infrastructure for the Dead Cities specifically was confirmed in this research. The standard overland route from Damascus runs north via Hama; Serjilla itself sits roughly 65 km north of Hama and about 80 km southwest of Aleppo, in the Jebel Riha hills — the Damascus-to-Hama leg distance was not independently confirmed here and should be checked against a current road map before publishing a specific figure.

## Getting there

Via Hama on the M5 highway (northbound from Damascus); Serjilla is roughly 65 km north of Hama and about 80 km southwest of Aleppo, in the Jebel Riha hills near al-Bara. Public transport reaches Idlib city and Aleppo; individual Dead Cities sites are reached from there by hired car, as no scheduled public transport to the sites themselves was found.

## Local flavour

Olive cultivation is the enduring thread connecting the modern region to the villages themselves: the Dead Cities' economy was built on olive-oil production (the ruins include numerous stone oil and wine presses), and Idlib province remains one of Syria's major olive-growing areas today, with olive oil, olives, and soap still central to the local table and economy.

## Corrections made to the submitted (placeholder) card

The submitted card contained no real content — every field read literally "placeholder" or was an empty array. There was nothing substantive to fact-check against; this document is a sourced rebuild. In building it, the following commonly-made errors about this specific site were deliberately avoided:

1. **Conflating the 2011 inscription year with the 2013 Danger List year.** These are frequently merged into one date in casual write-ups (one intermediate search summary used in this research did exactly that). They are reported separately here, each with its own source.
2. **Overstating the number of villages/sites with false precision.** Sources disagree — 36 formally inscribed villages, "around 40" in UNESCO's own summary description, and 700–800 across the wider Limestone Massif depending on what is being counted. The card reports this as a range rather than picking one number to sound authoritative.
3. **A specific area figure (12,290 hectares) surfaced in only one low-reliability intermediate source** and could not be corroborated by a second fetched source, so it was dropped rather than repeated.
4. **Softened superlatives.** A claim that a nearby basilica (Kharab Shams) is "one of the oldest, best-preserved Christian structures in the Levant" and an unqualified claim that Qalb Lozeh "inspired Notre-Dame Cathedral" both appeared in secondary sources; the latter is retained only as an attributed scholarly opinion (Gertrude Bell), not stated as settled fact, and the former was dropped rather than repeated.
5. **Used UNESCO's actual official Arabic name** (القرى القديمة في شمال سوريا) as the primary Arabic name, rather than the popular press/Wikipedia nickname "المدن المنسية" ("The Forgotten Cities") or "المدن الميتة" ("The Dead Cities"), both of which are noted only as popular alternate names.
6. **Omitted an unverifiable claim** that al-Bara specifically was "captured by HTS from government forces on 27 November 2024." Idlib's front lines shifted more than once between 2015 and 2020, so this claim is plausible but was not independently confirmed for al-Bara specifically, and it is not needed for a tourism card — omitted rather than asserted either way.
7. **Excluded claims found online about the modern religious/sectarian makeup of a village called "Qalb Loze/Qalb Lawzah,"** connected in some sources to a 2015 killing of Druze residents. It was not possible to confirm in this research whether that is the same village as the heritage site described here. Per this brief's tone guidance, no modern sectarian or demographic description of any living community is included anywhere in this card, regardless of source reliability.
8. **Avoided blanket "safe" or "dangerous" framing.** The access note cites the FCDO's actual current advisory wording rather than characterizing the country or region in binary terms.
9. **Described Qalb Lozeh and the Church of Saint Simeon Stylites as heritage architecture,** not curiosities, and did not claim either is an active place of worship today — sources indicate caretakers left in 2011 and no evidence of a current worshipping congregation at either site was found.
10. **Did not romanticize war damage.** Damage is described with what, when, and (where sourced) by whom, without aesthetic framing of ruins caused by conflict.
