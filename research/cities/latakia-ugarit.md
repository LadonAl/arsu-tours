# Latakia & Ugarit — Fact-Checked Tourism Card

**Slug:** `latakia-ugarit`
**Verdict: CORRECTED** — the original draft was largely well-sourced, but contained one significant factual error (the Latakia airport's name and operating status), one misdated conservation claim (Ugarit's "no looting" finding is from 2014, not 2025-2026), and several softer distance/superlative overstatements. Details below.

Arabic: اللاذقية وأوغاريت
Region: Latakia Governorate, Syria's Mediterranean coast — the port city of Latakia and the Ras Shamra headland 10 km to its north.

**One-liner:** Syria's Mediterranean coast, where the world's earliest known ordered alphabet was written into clay.

---

## Summary

Latakia is Syria's principal Mediterranean port and its fifth-largest city (est. population ~709,000 as of 2023), backed by pine-covered coastal mountains and fronted by a long corniche. Ten kilometres north, the low mound of Ras Shamra covers Ugarit, a Late Bronze Age port kingdom destroyed around 1185-1190 BCE during the wider collapse that ended the era across the eastern Mediterranean, likely amid Sea Peoples raids and famine (the last king's own letters describe enemy ships burning his cities). Ugarit is on Syria's UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List (submitted 6 August 1999), not yet a full inscribed World Heritage Site.

It is best known for the Ugaritic alphabet: a 30-sign cuneiform script first recovered from clay tablets on 14 May 1929 by Claude Schaeffer's French excavation mission (systematic digging at the site had begun that same year, after a farmer's 1928 plough accidentally opened an earlier tomb nearby). It is the earliest known systematically ordered alphabet, with a letter sequence closely paralleling the later Phoenician alphabet, though specialists debate exact primacy claims against other early Semitic scripts. Tablets recovered here include the Baal Cycle, a major cycle of Canaanite myth centred on the storm-god Baal-Hadad's conflicts with the sea (Yam) and death (Mot) for pre-eminence under the god El — presented here strictly as literary and religious heritage, not as any living cult.

North along the coast, the black-sand beaches of Ras al-Bassit sit about 53 km from Latakia, near Mount Aqra (Jebel Aqra), the limestone peak the Ugaritic texts identify as the storm-god's sacred mountain, "Sapan" (also transliterated Zaphon). The Armenian-Syrian mountain town of Kassab, about 59 km north of Latakia, and the nearby village of Salma offer cooler upland scenery historically popular with Syrian summer visitors. Latakia's own National Museum, housed in a converted 16th-century Ottoman tobacco khan, holds Ugarit tablets and other finds — but the building itself has been closed to prevent looting since 2011, with only its garden accessible. Tourism here is reopening cautiously after the December 2024 political transition, with new hotels, beach clubs and a dedicated tourism-police unit reported on the coast in 2026 — not a return to a settled pre-2011 normal, and both the US State Department and UK FCDO maintain formal "do not travel" advisories for Syria as a whole.

## Highlights

### Ugarit (Ras Shamra) — أوغاريت (رأس شمرا)
A low tell 10 km north of Latakia covering a Late Bronze Age port capital destroyed c. 1185-1190 BCE. Excavated since 1929; royal palace, temples, and residential quarters are exposed as ruins in an open-air site.
**Condition:** Ruins, excavated in phases since 1929. The most recent independently sourced condition assessment found in this research is from high-resolution satellite imagery dated **December 2011 and October 2014** (AAAS/Smithsonian analysis): it found the site "largely intact," with no erosion damage and no looting pits observed. A broader 2026 Dartmouth-led study of 200 Syrian archaeological sites found that 59% had suffered looting or mechanized damage between 2015 and 2025 (up from 13% in 2011-2016) — a serious national trend — but its published coverage does not specifically re-confirm Ugarit's individual status. Treat "still intact" as true as of the last verified (2014) assessment, not as an up-to-date 2026 finding.

### The Ugaritic alphabet tablets — الأبجدية الأوغاريتية
Clay tablets bearing a 30-sign cuneiform alphabet, first uncovered on 14 May 1929, legible from 1930. It is the earliest known systematically ordered alphabet — not proof of the single oldest alphabetic symbol ever devised, but the oldest known organized alphabetic sequence, closely paralleling later Phoenician letter order.
**Condition:** Originals dispersed to the Louvre and the National Museum of Damascus; local finds belong to Latakia's museum, currently closed to the public.

### The Baal Cycle tablets — ألواح دورة بعل
A set of Ugaritic tablets recording a major Canaanite myth cycle centred on the storm-god Baal-Hadad's conflicts with Yam (the sea) and Mot (death), for pre-eminence under the god El. Presented here strictly as ancient literary and religious heritage, not as any living cult or belief.
**Condition:** Tablets held in museum collections (Louvre, Damascus); not on physical display at the Ugarit site itself.

### National Museum of Latakia — المتحف الوطني باللاذقية
Housed in Khan al-Dukhan, a 16th-century Ottoman tobacco warehouse later used as the residence of the governor of the French Mandate–era Alawite State. Its six halls span Ugarit and Ebla through the Islamic period to contemporary art.
**Condition:** Closed to visitors since 2011 to protect the collection from looting; the courtyard and garden, with carved capitals and tombstones, remain accessible.

### Ras al-Bassit black-sand beaches — رأس البسيط
A cape about 53 km north of Latakia with dark volcanic-sand beaches, settled since the Bronze Age as a Ugaritic outpost, later the Greek/Roman port of Posideion. A traditional Syrian domestic seaside resort.
**Condition:** Open as a landscape/heritage site; pre-war it held several hundred chalets and visitor numbers reportedly reached around 150,000 a year by 2004, mostly Syrians from Aleppo and Damascus. Current (2026) facility condition was not independently confirmed in this research.

### Mount Aqra (Jebel Aqra) — جبل الأقرع
A 1,728 m limestone peak on the Syria–Turkey border near the mouth of the Orontes River. In Late Bronze Age Ugaritic religion it was Mount Sapan (also rendered Zaphon), the storm-god Baal's sacred mountain; today it is a scenic coastal-mountain landmark.
**Condition:** Accessible as a landscape feature. Sources disagree on its exact distance from Ugarit and Ras al-Bassit (one gives ~30 km north of Ugarit, another implies Ras al-Bassit sits ~10 km south of it) — treat any precise figure as approximate. Its "highest peak on the eastern Mediterranean coast" billing is a commonly repeated claim that could not be independently verified against an authoritative ranking in this research; formal hiking infrastructure is also unverified.

### Kassab and Salma — كسب وسلمى
Kassab, about 59 km north of Latakia at roughly 750-800 m elevation, is a historically Armenian, predominantly Christian summer resort town on the slope of Mount Aqra. Nearby Salma is another coastal-mountain village long popular with Syrian summer visitors.
**Condition:** Both saw fighting and population displacement during the civil war (Kassab notably in 2014); current tourist-readiness not independently confirmed for 2026.

**UNESCO status:** Tentative List only (submitted 1999, listed by UNESCO as "Ugrarit / Tell Shamra"), not a full UNESCO World Heritage Site and not on the List of World Heritage in Danger under that name. Syria's six existing full World Heritage Sites — the Ancient City of Damascus, Site of Palmyra, Ancient City of Bosra, Ancient City of Aleppo, Crac des Chevaliers and Qal'at Salah El-Din, and the Ancient Villages of Northern Syria — were separately placed on the Danger List in June 2013; Ugarit itself is not among them.

**Conservation note:** Ugarit's most recently *independently verified* condition assessment found in this research dates to satellite imagery from December 2011 and October 2014 (AAAS/Smithsonian coverage), which found the tell largely intact with no visible looting pits or erosion damage — in contrast to the situation at many other Syrian sites. A separate, broader Dartmouth-led study published in 2026 (in the journal *Heritage*) surveyed 200 Syrian archaeological sites and found 59% showed looting or mechanized damage between 2015 and 2025 (versus 13% in 2011-2016), with the worst cases concentrated at sites such as Palmyra, Apamea, and Mari; its published coverage does not specifically re-confirm Ugarit's individual status as of 2025-2026. Readers should not assume Ugarit's 2014 condition still holds today — only that no reporting of damage there specifically was found in this research. The National Museum of Latakia, separately, has been closed to the public since 2011 specifically to protect its Ugarit tablets and other holdings from theft; only its courtyard is open. No large-scale mechanized destruction has been reported nationally since the government fell in December 2024, which researchers cite as a cautiously positive sign, not a declaration that recovery is complete.

**Access note:** As of September 2026, both the US State Department (Level 4, "Do Not Travel") and the UK FCDO ("advises against all travel to Syria," advice updated 2 September 2026) maintain formal advisories citing unpredictable security conditions, terrorism risk, and the absence of reliable consular support inside the country — this is a formal advisory status, not a blanket claim that the coast itself is unsafe day to day. Ras Shamra/Ugarit is described by regional sources as an open, visitable archaeological site near Latakia, and Latakia's own corniche and beaches are reported (Monocle, 2026) as actively reopening, with a new private beach club (Hola), the Afamia Hotel Resort, and a newly formed 225-officer tourism-police unit (including 50 women) now patrolling beaches and tourist sites. **Latakia's airport is not currently a viable arrival point**: it has been renamed Latakia International Airport (dropping the Assad-family name) but, as of the most recent reporting found (March 2026), remains closed to civilian traffic with no scheduled airlines, amid unresolved Syria–Russia negotiations over the adjacent military airbase. The National Museum of Latakia building remains closed; only its exterior grounds are open. Conditions at Ras al-Bassit, Kassab, and Salma specifically in 2026 were not independently verified beyond general governorate-level sourcing — travellers should check current conditions before planning a coastal-mountain leg beyond Latakia city.

**Best season:** Late spring (April-June) and early autumn (September-October) for mild coastal weather; July-August is the traditional Syrian beach-resort high season at Ras al-Bassit and Kassab but hot and crowded; Kassab's elevation makes it the traditional summer escape from coastal heat.

**Getting there:** Damascus to Latakia is roughly 310-320 km by road via the M5 and M4 motorways, a baseline drive of around 4 hours under normal conditions (current checkpoint and security conditions along the route were not independently verified for 2026, so actual travel time may differ). Air access via Latakia's own airport is not currently available — see the access note above; travellers should check current routing (e.g. via Damascus International Airport) rather than assume direct flights to Latakia.

**Local flavour:** Latakia and the coast are known for Mediterranean-leaning Syrian cuisine — fresh grilled fish, olive oil and citrus from the surrounding groves, and the coastal mezze table — reflecting the governorate's dense olive and citrus orchards; Kassab is additionally known for its distinct Armenian culinary and craft traditions within Syria. (Not independently re-verified against a dedicated food-culture source in this session; carried over from general regional knowledge.)

## Corrections made to the original draft

1. **Airport name and status (major).** Original: "Bassel al-Assad International Airport with some domestic and limited international connections." Corrected: the airport was renamed **Latakia International Airport** after the Assad government fell in December 2024, and — per the most recent reporting found (March 2026) — remains **closed to civilian traffic with no airlines operating**, due to unresolved Syria–Russia negotiations over the adjacent Khmeimim air base. The original claim was both outdated on the name and wrong on operating status; this is corrected throughout (highlights table, gettingThere, accessNote).
2. **Ugarit's "2025-2026 satellite survey found no looting" claim (major).** Original attributed a "no looting pits, largely intact" finding to 2025-2026 surveys covered by Dartmouth/Smithsonian/AAAS/phys.org. Verified: the specific "no looting pits" finding for Ugarit comes from a 2011/2014 AAAS/Smithsonian satellite assessment, not 2025-2026. The real 2026 Dartmouth study (published in *Heritage*) is a separate, broader 200-site survey that found 59% of sites looted or damaged 2015-2025 — a figure the original card correctly cited — but its published coverage does not specifically reconfirm Ugarit's current status. Rewrote the conservation note to keep these two facts distinct and correctly dated, rather than implying a fresh 2025-2026 all-clear for Ugarit specifically.
3. **Damascus–Latakia road distance/time.** Original: "roughly 350 km / a 4.5-5 hour drive." Verified baseline (route-planner data, undisrupted conditions): ~310-320 km, ~4 hours via the M5/M4 motorways. Corrected the figure and kept the caveat that current checkpoint/security conditions were not independently verified.
4. **Mount Aqra superlative.** Original stated flatly that it is "the highest peak on the eastern Mediterranean coast." This could not be verified against an authoritative source; flagged as an unverified, commonly repeated claim rather than presented as fact. Added its actual elevation (1,728 m) for concreteness.
5. **Mount Aqra distance figures.** Original implied it sits "a few kilometres north of Ras al-Bassit." Sources conflict (Wikipedia's Jebel Aqra article gives ~30 km north of Ugarit; the Ras al-Bassit article implies Mount Aqra is only ~10 km further north than Ras al-Bassit itself, i.e., much closer to Ugarit than 30 km would allow). Flagged the discrepancy instead of asserting a single unverifiable figure.
6. **Ras al-Bassit visitor-numbers phrasing.** Original: "roughly 150,000 mostly-Syrian visitors a year." Source actually states visitor numbers "reached 150,000 by 2004" — reworded to avoid implying this was a steady, ongoing annual figure rather than a peak reached by a particular pre-war year.
7. **Kassab elevation.** Original range: "650-850 m." Verified sources give ~750-800 m; tightened the range accordingly.
8. **Added Latakia population figure** (~709,000, 2023 estimate) as supporting evidence for the "fifth-largest city" claim, which was otherwise asserted without a cited number.
9. **Confirmed unchanged (no correction needed):** Ugarit's destruction date (c. 1185 BCE / Late Bronze Age collapse), the 14 May 1929 tablet-discovery date and Schaeffer's role, the 10 km Ugarit–Latakia distance, the 53 km Ras al-Bassit distance, the 59 km Kassab distance, the UNESCO Tentative List submission date and UNESCO's own "Ugrarit" typo in its site title, the National Museum's 1986 founding / 2011 closure / Alawite State governor's-residence history, the Baal Cycle description, the 59%-of-200-sites Dartmouth looting statistic, the six 2013 Danger-Listed Syrian World Heritage Sites, and the current US/UK travel advisory statuses and the Monocle 2026 reporting on Latakia's reopening (beach club, Afamia Hotel Resort, 225-officer tourism-police unit) — all independently verified against primary or near-primary sources in this session.
