# Maaloula (معلولا)

**Region:** Rif Dimashq Governorate, Qalamoun mountains, ~56 km northeast of Damascus
**One-liner:** A cliffside village in the Qalamoun where Western Neo-Aramaic is still spoken today.

## Summary

Maaloula is built into a limestone gorge in the Qalamoun mountains of Rif Dimashq, at an altitude of roughly 1,500 metres. It is one of three places on earth — alongside the neighbouring villages of Jubb'adin and Bakh'a — where Western Neo-Aramaic is still spoken as a living language, a direct descendant of the Aramaic spoken in the region in the first centuries CE. Jubb'adin, often assumed to be a minor satellite of Maaloula, is in fact the larger of the two: its 2004 census population (3,778) already exceeded Maaloula's (2,762), and recent estimates put it at 8,000–10,000 residents against Maaloula's roughly 500 today.

The village grew up around a dramatic natural cleft in the rock — locally called "the Fajj" — which local tradition ties to the story of Saint Thecla, and around two monastic communities: the Melkite Greek Catholic Monastery of Mar Sarkis (Saints Sergius and Bacchus), built on the site of an earlier temple, and the Greek Orthodox Convent of Mar Taqla (Saint Thecla). Both remain active places of worship, not museum pieces.

Maaloula changed hands repeatedly during the Syrian conflict. Al-Nusra Front and allied opposition groups first took the town in an assault beginning September 4, 2013; government forces recaptured it around September 15. A second, brief occupation followed in late October 2013. The town fell a third time on December 2–3, 2013, when twelve nuns from Mar Taqla were taken hostage and held for roughly three months before their release in March 2014; this was the longest of the occupations. Syrian government forces, with Hezbollah support, retook Maaloula for good on April 14, 2014. Fighting, looting and shelling during these episodes damaged religious buildings and destroyed or stole icons; much of the town emptied.

Restoration of specific structures — including Mar Taqla — has proceeded gradually since 2014, with support from a mix of Syrian, church and international donors (Mar Taqla's rebuild drew significant support from a Russian veterans' organization, Boevoe Bratstvo). But the village's pre-war population and visitor numbers have not come close to recovering, and the period since the fall of the Assad government in December 2024 has brought a new layer of uncertainty rather than a clean resolution. As of 2026, Maaloula's monasteries hold services again and day trips from Damascus are reported as routine, but the security picture at the village level has been described as fragile at times since the transition, and government "do not travel" advisories for Syria as a whole remain in force. Its Aramaic heritage is the subject of active, and by some accounts urgent, preservation efforts.

## Highlights

### Monastery of Mar Sarkis (Saints Sergius and Bacchus) — دير مار سركيس
A Melkite Greek Catholic monastery whose core is described by multiple sources as dating to roughly the 5th–6th century, built above the remains of an earlier temple — among the oldest surviving monasteries in Syria (per Wikipedia; not independently verified against a specialist source). It held a collection of Byzantine-era icons before 2013, including one depicting the Last Supper; most were looted or destroyed during the 2013 occupation, and the building itself sustained shelling damage (walls, dome, altar). It is an active place of worship; because Aramaic speakers have dwindled, Mass is now generally said in Arabic, though the Lord's Prayer is often still recited in Aramaic.
**Condition:** damaged and looted in the 2013 occupation; services resumed, restoration reported ongoing/completed in stages since 2014 — full physical-restoration status not independently confirmed.

### Convent of Mar Taqla (Saint Thecla) — دير مار تقلا
A Greek Orthodox convent built into the rock, venerating Saint Thecla, traditionally linked to the apostle Paul. Twelve of its nuns were abducted on December 2–3, 2013, and held for about three months before release on March 9, 2014. It remains a working convent and pilgrimage site.
**Condition:** significantly damaged during the 2013 occupation; restoration (backed in part by a Russian veterans' organization) reported around 90% complete by mid-2018, with nuns back in residence; reopened to visitors and pilgrims.

### The gorge (the Fajj) — الفج
A narrow natural passage through the cliff face — roughly 300 m long, 20–30 m deep — that gives the village its striking silhouette; the village's own name is traditionally derived from an Aramaic word for "entrance." Local tradition associates the cleft with Saint Thecla's flight from persecution. It remains the village's defining physical feature.
**Condition:** intact; a walkable natural feature, not war-affected.

### Village houses and old town — البيوت القديمة
Stone houses built directly into the cliff, many washed in pale blue paint in a local tradition honouring the Virgin Mary. Some homes were destroyed or abandoned during the 2013–2014 fighting and their owners have not returned; housing reconstruction has lagged behind the monasteries'.
**Condition:** mixed — many restored and inhabited, others still in poor repair; exact current figures not available from sources checked.

## UNESCO status

Not inscribed as a World Heritage Site. On UNESCO's Tentative List since 8 June 1999 (criteria v, vi), listed simply as "Maaloula." It has never been formally inscribed and is not on the List of World Heritage in Danger — that Danger List entry (added 2013) applies to Syria's six already-inscribed sites as a group; Maaloula was never inscribed in the first place, so it cannot appear on the Danger List regardless of its condition.

## Conservation note

Maaloula suffered real, well-documented war damage. Al-Nusra Front and allied fighters occupied the town repeatedly in 2013, used Mar Sarkis as a base, looted or destroyed Byzantine-era icons, shelled religious buildings, and damaged caves and cemeteries; twelve nuns from Mar Taqla were held hostage for about three months. Recovery since 2014 has been real but uneven. Mar Taqla's rebuild — backed partly by a Russian veterans' organization — was reported around 90% complete in 2018, with nuns back in residence; Mar Sarkis has also resumed services, now generally in Arabic rather than Aramaic because so few fluent speakers remain. But housing reconstruction has lagged behind the monasteries, some residents who fled have not returned, and Maaloula's Aramaic-teaching institute has had no active instructor since the death of its last teacher, Georges Ruzqalla, in 2021. The post-December-2024 transition added a further layer of uncertainty: reporting from early 2025 described a village left without a permanent security presence and a majority of residents temporarily relocated to Damascus, while heritage-focused reporting through mid-2026 describes recovery and renewed tourist interest without giving hard numbers. Treat any claim of "full restoration" as unverified — the honest picture is partial, ongoing recovery in a still-changing environment, not completion.

## Access note

Day trips to Maaloula from Damascus are reported as routine in 2026 sources, and the monasteries function as active places of worship that welcome respectful visitors. That said, this needs qualification rather than a blanket assurance: as of recent searches, the US, UK and Australian governments maintain "Do Not Travel" (Level 4-equivalent) advisories for Syria as a whole, entry requires a visa arranged through a tour operator, and early-2025 reporting specifically described Maaloula as left without a permanent security post after the government transition, with many residents temporarily relocated to Damascus. More recent 2026 tourism coverage (SANA, Arab News) treats Maaloula as an active, recovering heritage destination, but exact current opening hours, permit requirements, and visitor volumes for Maaloula specifically could not be confirmed from a dated, authoritative source. Confirm current conditions locally or through a tour operator before travel. Visitor volumes remain far below pre-war levels — one 2025 source cited roughly 6,000 annual visitors to the wider area versus 200,000 in 2010 — so facilities, signage and guide availability may be limited compared to before 2011.

## Best season

Spring (April–May) and autumn (September–October) for mild mountain temperatures; winters are cold at altitude. Pre-war accounts describe summers as busy with Damascus day-trippers — with visitor numbers still a small fraction of 2010 levels, it's unclear how much that pattern still holds today.

## Local flavour

The Qalamoun hillsides around Maaloula are associated with vineyards and olive and apricot orchards, which sources describe as part of what has sustained the village economically. Specific claims about arak or wine production, or about particular village dishes, are general regional association rather than something independently confirmed against a 2025–2026 source about Maaloula specifically — treat as indicative, not confirmed current fact.
