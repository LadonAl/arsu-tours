## الاسم / Names

- **English:** Tartus & Arwad Island
- **Arabic:** طرطوس وجزيرة أرواد
- **Region:** Tartus Governorate, Syrian Mediterranean coast

## One-liner

A Crusader cathedral-museum on shore, and Syria's only inhabited island three kilometres out to sea.

## Summary

Tartus grew from the Phoenician colony of Antaradus ("opposite Arados") into the Crusader stronghold of Tortosa, and is today Syria's second-largest port city (after Latakia). Its old quarter still sits inside the moat and walls of the 12th-century Crusader fortress, and at its centre stands the fortified Cathedral of Our Lady of Tortosa: built in 1123 over an earlier Byzantine church, held by the Knights Templar from 1152, converted after the Muslim reconquest into a mosque and later used by the Ottomans as a barracks/storage building, then renovated under the French Mandate and turned into the National Museum of Tartus in 1956.

Both Tartus (as "The City-fortress of the Crusaders") and its offshore neighbour Arwad — Syria's only inhabited island, the ancient Phoenician city-state of Arados — have sat on Syria's UNESCO Tentative List since 1999. Arwad, about 3 km from the mainland and still ringed by sections of Phoenician-era harbour wall, remains a working fishing and boat-building community. Just south of Tartus, the site of Amrit (ancient Marathus) preserves a rock-cut Phoenician sanctuary and two funerary towers known as al-Maghazel, "the Spindles" — a site that has also suffered real, documented looting during the conflict years, detailed below. Inland, the Templar keep at Safita — the "White Tower," Chastel Blanc — still has an active Greek Orthodox chapel on its ground floor.

As of September 2026, Syria's tourism authorities are actively promoting the coast, and the Tartus–Arwad ferry gateway (Al Tahouna Port) is reported under infrastructure upgrade — but access, site openings, and on-the-ground conditions are changing month to month, and the country carries a blanket "advise against all travel" designation from at least one government (see Access note).

## Highlights

### Cathedral of Our Lady of Tortosa / National Museum of Tartus
كاتدرائية سيدة طرطوس / متحف طرطوس

Built by Crusaders in 1123 over an earlier Byzantine church, this fortified cathedral is often described by historians as among the best-preserved Crusader religious buildings anywhere (an attributed, not independently verified, superlative). After the Muslim reconquest it served as a mosque, then under Ottoman rule as a barracks/storage building; it was renovated under the French Mandate and became Tartus's archaeological museum in 1956.

**Condition:** Closed to visitors for restoration and structural/conservation work, per Syrian state media (SANA), 22 May 2026 — no reopening date given. The building also sustained new cracking to its decorative columns in the February 2023 Turkey–Syria earthquake (see Conservation note). The current restoration is reported to be tied to a July 2026 cooperation agreement between Syria's Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums (DGAM) and the Aga Khan Trust for Culture.

### Old City of Tartus
المدينة القديمة في طرطوس

A dense quarter of later buildings raised on and inside the walls of the original Crusader fortress, with the old fortress moat still tracing the boundary between the historic core and the modern city around it.

**Condition:** Inhabited and intact; an ordinary functioning neighbourhood, not a roped-off site.

### Arwad Island
جزيرة أرواد

Syria's only inhabited island and the ancient Phoenician city-state of Arados, reached by a short boat crossing from Tartus. Sections of Phoenician-era harbour wall still stand, and local shipwrights continue a wooden boat-building tradition.

**Condition:** Inhabited, functioning fishing and boat-building town. The ferry gateway, Al Tahouna Port, is reported under infrastructure upgrade in 2026 (expanded docking capacity, modernised safety systems) ahead of the summer tourism season.

### Amrit (ancient Marathus) — the Ma'abed
عمريت - المعبد

A Phoenician sanctuary cut directly into bedrock, its court measuring roughly 47 by 49 metres and more than 3 metres deep, dedicated to the gods Melqart and Eshmun. Excavated by archaeologists in 1955–1957.

**Condition:** Open archaeological site; the rock-cut court and central cella are substantially preserved and it is managed by DGAM. It is not undamaged, however: a Roman-era hypogeum on the site was damaged in the February 2023 earthquake, and the Azar cemetery area of the site has been repeatedly looted with heavy machinery — mapped by DGAM in 2013, and reported recurring in 2012 and again in 2025 by a peer-reviewed 2026 study. See Conservation note.

### The Spindle Towers (al-Maghazel), Amrit
المغازل

Two funerary towers marking underground burial chambers in Amrit's necropolis: cylindrical stone forms roughly 7–7.5 metres tall — one topped by a damaged pyramid, the other by lion figures near its base (the latter apparently left unfinished).

**Condition:** Both towers still stand above ground, weathered but structurally present. Their exact date is disputed between sources (see Risky claims).

### Safita White Tower (Chastel Blanc)
برج صافيتا الأبيض

A Knights Templar keep roughly 28 metres tall with walls about 3 metres thick, held by the Templars by 1152. It was captured and largely destroyed by the Mamluk sultan Baibars in 1271, and later underwent major structural repair in 1946. Its ground floor holds a chapel dedicated to St. Michael that remains an active place of worship for Safita's Greek Orthodox community today.

**Condition:** Intact and standing. Some sources report new cracking in the tower from the February 2023 earthquake, alongside a partial collapse at a separate, smaller tower in Safita (Umm Hosh); I could not independently confirm the extent on the White Tower itself via a primary source I could fetch directly (see Conservation note). The chapel is in active religious use — visit it as a living church, not a curiosity.

## UNESCO status

Tartus (listed as "The City-fortress of the Crusaders") and the Island of Arwad ("L'île d'Arwad") have both been on Syria's UNESCO Tentative List since 1999, as cultural sites. Neither has been formally inscribed as a full World Heritage Site, and neither is on the List of World Heritage in Danger. Amrit is not separately tentative-listed. I was not able to directly fetch the whc.unesco.org tentative-list pages themselves (403 Forbidden on both attempts, same as the original researcher found); the above is corroborated across multiple independent search-indexed sources but not verified against the UNESCO primary document text.

## Conservation note (corrected)

The Cathedral of Our Lady of Tortosa / National Museum of Tartus has been repurposed repeatedly across history — mosque, Ottoman barracks/storage, museum since 1956 — and Syrian state media reported it closed for structural restoration as of 22 May 2026, tied to a July 2026 conservation agreement with the Aga Khan Trust for Culture, with no reopening date given.

Unlike the original version of this card, which could not confirm site-specific damage, real damage is documented at two of these sites:

- **February 2023 earthquake (M7.8 Turkey–Syria earthquake and aftershocks):** Syrian state media reported new cracking in the decorative columns of the Tartus cathedral/museum and cracks in the Safita ("White") Tower, plus a partial collapse at a separate, smaller tower in Safita (Umm Hosh), and damage to a Roman-era hypogeum at Amrit. I found this via SANA's archive and Syria Times reporting from February 2023, corroborated by a search-indexed abstract of a 2026 peer-reviewed study; both primary articles timed out or refused connection on direct fetch, so treat the Safita/Tartus-cathedral earthquake detail as well-corroborated but not independently primary-verified by me.
- **Illegal excavation at Amrit:** DGAM's own 2013 site mapping identified illegal excavation in Amrit's Azar cemetery area, and a peer-reviewed study published 9 February 2026 ("Beyond the battlefield: assessing cultural heritage damage in Tartus, Syria," Journal of Eastern Mediterranean Archaeology and Heritage Studies, DOI 10.1080/00758914.2026.2615592) reports the same area looted again in 2012 and again as recently as 2025. I could not fetch the full article directly (403 Forbidden, paywalled); this is drawn from the article's search-indexed abstract and secondary summaries, not the primary text.
- I found no fetchable, site-specific account of active-conflict (2011–2024) shelling or military use at Tartus's Old City, Arwad, or the Safita tower specifically — one AAAS satellite-imagery assessment explicitly defers Tartus and Arwad to an unpublished second report, and a UNESCO 2016 state-of-conservation note (search-indexed only) describes "no visible damage" at the Tartus citadel-city site at that time. Treat that absence of reported war damage as "not found," not as a certification that none occurred.

## Access note

The UK FCDO advises against all travel to Syria as a whole country, without a regional carve-out for the coast (advice last updated 2 September 2026, confirmed current as of this check) — check your own government's current advisory before planning a visit; this is not a claim that the coast is unsafe or safe, only that no official source I found differentiates it regionally.

Syria's tourism ministry publicly launched its 2026 summer tourism season in June 2026, centred on Tartus and the coast, and reported infrastructure upgrades to Al Tahouna Port, the ferry gateway to Arwad (expanded docking capacity, modernised safety systems). The Cathedral/Museum in Tartus was closed for restoration as of the most recent report I could verify (22 May 2026) — confirm locally before planning around it. Amrit is an open, DGAM-managed site, though parts of it (the Azar cemetery area) have a documented history of illegal excavation, which is a heritage-protection concern rather than a visitor-safety one. The Safita tower's ground-floor chapel is an active church; visit respectfully as you would any functioning place of worship, not purely as a monument.

## Local flavour

The Syrian coast is known for seafood built around the day's catch — grilled fish and dishes like sayadieh (spiced fish and rice) are widely associated with coastal Syrian cooking generally — and Arwad's identity is inseparable from its wooden boat-building craft, with local shipwrights still building vessels using techniques passed down for generations. I have moderate but not source-verified confidence on the specific "Tartus-area" framing of sayadieh (it's a broader Levantine-coast dish); the boat-building tradition is well corroborated across multiple sources.

## Best season

Spring (April–May) and early autumn (September–October) for milder Mediterranean coastal weather. Syria's official 2026 "summer tourism season" launched in June 2026 and is when infrastructure and events are most active on the coast, but this is a government tourism-promotion push, not independent travel-conditions reporting, and summer on this coast is hot.

## Getting there

Tartus is roughly 250–255 km from Damascus by road via the coastal M5 highway (corrected from the original card's "~220 km" — mapping sources consistently show driving distance in the 250s, not 220; straight-line/air distance is shorter, about 157–159 km, which is likely what produced the lower figure). Historically a drive of about 3–3.5 hours. Arwad is reached by a short boat crossing (minutes, not hours) from Tartus's Al Tahouna Port. I was not able to verify current road conditions, checkpoint situations, or which airports (Damascus International vs. Latakia's Bassel Al-Assad International) currently offer practical routing to Tartus as of September 2026 — treat specific transit logistics as unconfirmed and subject to change.

## Risky claims (carried over / updated)

- Arwad's population figure (4,403) is from the 2004 census — over two decades old — and almost certainly does not reflect the island's current population; informal estimates of 5,000–10,000 circulate but are not tied to an official count.
- The Tartus Museum's closure for restoration is confirmed only as of a 22 May 2026 report; no source confirms whether it has reopened or remains closed as of September 2026.
- Sources disagree on the dating of Amrit's Spindle Towers: some material implies a 6th–5th century BCE (Achaemenid-period) date via associated burial-chamber finds, while other secondary sources give a 4th century BCE date; I have not resolved this discrepancy against a primary archaeological publication.
- Descriptions of Amrit's ma'abed as "the best-preserved Phoenician sanctuary in the Mediterranean" are a commonly repeated secondary-source superlative, not an independently verified academic claim — I have deliberately left this superlative out of the corrected card. (The cathedral's "one of the best-preserved Crusader religious buildings anywhere" is the same kind of claim; I kept it but attributed it explicitly to "historians" rather than stating it as fact — treat it with the same caution.)
- The Ma'abed's construction date is itself disputed. The original card dated it "around the late 5th century BCE"; one source I found gives late 4th century BCE instead, and my own Wikipedia fetch gave no specific century at all. I removed the date claim from the corrected card rather than repeat either unresolved figure.
- I could not directly fetch the UNESCO tentative-list pages for Tartus or Arwad (whc.unesco.org returned 403 Forbidden on repeated attempts) or the full academic article on Tartus-area heritage damage (tandfonline.com returned 403 Forbidden / timed out); the UNESCO facts and the 2023-earthquake and Amrit-looting details above rely on search-indexed snippets and secondary reporting (SANA, Syria Times, search-engine abstract synthesis), not the full primary documents.
- I could not verify current, specific security or checkpoint conditions on the Damascus–Tartus–Amrit–Safita route beyond the blanket, country-wide FCDO advisory, which does not distinguish the coast from other regions.
- "Second-largest port city" for Tartus is corroborated by multiple sources describing it as Syria's second port after Latakia, but I could not independently confirm this via a population-ranked city list; treat it as a reasonably well-supported but not airtight descriptor.
- Local-cuisine claims (sayadieh as characteristic of the Tartus area specifically) reflect general knowledge of Levantine coastal cooking rather than a source I fetched and verified for this task.

## Corrections made (from the original card)

1. **Damascus–Tartus distance corrected upward.** Original said "roughly 220 km." Multiple current mapping sources (distance.to, travelmath) give a driving distance in the 250–255 km range via the M5 coastal highway; the straight-line/air distance is about 157–159 km, which is likely the figure that got conflated with road distance. Corrected the region field and "Getting there" section; kept the 3–3.5 hour drive-time estimate, which is roughly consistent with 250 km on a highway route.
2. **Added dated, site-specific damage the original card said it could not find.** The original card's conservationNote said "I found no fetchable, site-specific account of war damage to the cathedral, Amrit, Arwad, or the Safita tower." On checking further, I found (via secondary/search-indexed sources, since the primary articles were paywalled or unreachable): February 2023 earthquake cracking reported at both the Tartus cathedral/museum and the Safita tower, a partial collapse at a separate Safita tower (Umm Hosh), earthquake damage to a Roman hypogeum at Amrit, and a documented, recurring pattern of illegal excavation at Amrit's Azar cemetery (DGAM-mapped in 2013, reported again in 2012 and 2025 by a 2026 academic study). This is more specific and more concerning than "unconfirmed," and belongs in the card — added to the Conservation note and to Amrit's and the cathedral's "condition" fields, with appropriate sourcing caveats since I could not fetch the primary articles directly.
3. **Softened the Ottoman-era "arms depot" claim.** Wikipedia's own account (and corroborating search results) describes the cathedral's Ottoman-era use as a barracks/storage building rather than specifically an "arms depot." Changed the highlight blurb and summary wording accordingly; this is a minor precision fix, not a factual reversal.
4. **Added the Baibars 1271 destruction of Chastel Blanc.** The original card said the tower was "restored in 1946" without explaining why; Wikipedia records that it was captured and largely destroyed by the Mamluk sultan Baibars in 1271, which is the reason it needed major structural repair centuries later. Added for historical accuracy and so "restored" doesn't read as unexplained.
5. **Clarified the Tartus population figure's basis.** The 458,327 (2023) figure is a city-level estimate (Macrotrends/World Population Review methodology), not a census count, and is much smaller than the governorate-wide population including IDPs (~1.2 million per IOM, March 2025). Added this distinction so the number isn't read as more authoritative than it is; I also could not independently confirm this exact figure sits in the Wikipedia infobox itself, only that it is a commonly search-indexed figure attributed to Tartus.
6. **Removed the unsourced Phoenician-sanctuary superlative.** Left out "best-preserved Phoenician sanctuary in the Mediterranean" entirely rather than repeating it with a disclaimer, per the brief's instruction that an empty claim beats a wrong one — this one adds nothing verifiable.
7. **Tightened the Spindle Tower height.** Original said "roughly 7 metres"; Wikipedia gives approximately 7.5 m for at least one tower. Changed to "7–7.5 metres" to reflect the range rather than a single rounded figure.
8. **Added the specific restoration program name.** The May 2026 SANA report on the museum closure connects to a July 2026 cooperation agreement between DGAM and the Aga Khan Trust for Culture (confirmed via a second SANA article, sana.sy/en/culture-and-arts/2331522) — added as useful, concrete context for "when might this reopen."
