# Aleppo (حلب) — Fact-Checked City Card

**Slug:** aleppo
**Verdict:** Corrected (two factual errors fixed, several claims independently confirmed, tone strengthened for living places of worship)

---

## Overview

**Region:** Aleppo Governorate, northwestern Syria, near the Turkish border
**One-liner:** One of the world's oldest continuously inhabited cities, rebuilding souq by souq

Aleppo (Halab) is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities on earth, with settlement traces reaching back millennia and a historic core inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1986. Its Old City — the Citadel, the Great Umayyad Mosque, kilometers of vaulted souqs, Ottoman-era khans, and the mixed-confession quarter of Jdeideh — was badly damaged during the 2012–2016 Battle of Aleppo, when an estimated 30% or more of the historic fabric was destroyed or gutted, and the site was placed on UNESCO's List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013, where it remains as of the 2025 State of Conservation report. The February 2023 Turkey-Syria earthquake added fresh damage: it collapsed the western tower of the Old City's defensive wall and part of the Citadel's Ottoman mill, and cracked the Citadel's mosque minaret and entrance complex.

Since roughly 2017, and accelerating after 2019, UNESCO, the Aga Khan Trust for Culture, the Syrian Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums, and local craftsmen have restored large sections of Al-Madina Souq, parts of the Citadel, and historic houses in Jdeideh; the souq reopened to shopkeepers through 2024, with well over 300 shops trading again. Formal reconstruction of the Great Mosque's minaret — destroyed in April 2013 — resumed on 30 November 2025 and is ongoing. This is a city in active, unfinished recovery: visitors will see both painstakingly rebuilt heritage and sites still fenced off or under scaffolding, alongside living communities of worship that never stopped using their mosques and churches even through the worst damage.

## Highlights

### Citadel of Aleppo (قلعة حلب)
A fortified mound at the city's heart with occupation layers reaching to the first millennium BC; most visible structures date to the 13th-century Ayyubid period. Damaged in the February 2023 earthquake — the entrance tower's arch and tiling cracked, stones fell from the gate, and sections of the north, east, south and west defensive walls came down, alongside cracks and a collapsed spire on the Citadel's own mosque minaret.
**Condition:** Partially restored; the Culture Ministry reopened it to the public after stabilizing the southern tower and entrance, but earthquake repair work continues in other sections as of 2025–2026.

### Great Umayyad Mosque of Aleppo (الجامع الأموي الكبير)
An active mosque founded in the early 8th century CE under the Umayyad caliph Sulayman (r. 715–717), with a landmark minaret begun in 1090. The minaret was destroyed in an exchange of heavy weapons fire on 24 April 2013; the Syrian government blamed Jabhat al-Nusra for detonating explosives, while opposition sources attributed it to Syrian Army tank fire — responsibility remains genuinely disputed between the parties. Formal reconstruction of the minaret resumed on 30 November 2025, using recovered original stone fragments wherever possible.
**Condition:** Functioning as a mosque and briefly reopened for public prayer during Ramadan (March 2025); minaret rebuild ongoing, surrounding restoration work continues.

### Al-Madina Souq (سوق المدينة)
Historically one of the largest covered markets in the Middle East, with a network of vaulted stone passageways running many kilometers. Heavily destroyed in the war (closed roughly 2015–2021); restoration backed by UNESCO and the Aga Khan Trust for Culture reopened successive sections through 2024, with reporting describing well over 300 shops trading again by the end of that year.
**Condition:** Partially restored and reopened, with active commerce in several sections; other sections remain closed or mid-restoration.

### Khan al-Wazir and the historic khans
Ottoman-era caravanserais anchored Aleppo's trade routes: Khan al-Wazir, endowed in 1682 by the grand vizier Merzifonlu Kara Mustafa Pasha; Khan al-Shouneh, built 1546, now a market for traditional Aleppine handicrafts; and Khan al-Jumrok (the customs khan), built 1574 and among the largest khans in the old city, whose facade needed urgent stabilization after the 2023 earthquake.
**Condition:** Mixed — some stabilized and accessible, others still under repair.

### Jdeideh Quarter (الجديدة)
A quarter established by Aleppo's Christian residents just north of the city walls in the early 1420s, known for carved stone mansions such as Beit Wakil and Beit Ghazaleh. Its Forty Martyrs Cathedral — the seat of the Armenian Apostolic Archdiocese of Beroea and one of the oldest active churches still in use in the city — was completed in 1491, replacing an earlier chapel; an adjoining, older structure on the same site (now the Zarehian Treasury museum) predates 1429. The cathedral complex was badly hit by explosions under nearby al-Hatab Square on 28 April 2015 — the main sanctuary and bell tower survived, but surrounding buildings were destroyed — and it was restored and reconsecrated in March 2019.
**Condition:** Largely restored; the cathedral remains an active place of worship, and some historic houses have been converted into museums, restaurants and small hotels.

### Baron Hotel (فندق البارون)
Syria's first Western-style hotel, built by Armenian brothers Armenak and Onnig Mazloumian, with its ground floor completed in 1909 and full two-floor opening in 1911. Guests reportedly included T.E. Lawrence and Agatha Christie, who is said locally to have written part of *Murder on the Orient Express* in Room 203 — a widely repeated claim from the hotel's own tradition that could not be independently corroborated in academic or archival sources. The hotel closed its doors in November 2014 as the front line reached the building, though it informally sheltered refugees afterward; government forces retook eastern Aleppo in December 2016. Its long-term future remains unresolved.
**Condition:** Closed and deteriorating, not open to visitors as of 2026.

## UNESCO Status

Inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1986 (criteria iii, iv) as part of the "Ancient City of Aleppo." Placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013 due to armed conflict, and confirmed still on that list in UNESCO's most recent (2025) State of Conservation report; a "Desired State of Conservation for Removal" has not yet been finalized.

## Conservation Note

Aleppo's Old City sustained major war damage between 2012 and 2016 and further structural damage in the February 2023 earthquake, which cracked defensive walls, collapsed a tower of the Old City wall and part of the Citadel's Ottoman mill, and weakened souq buildings and khans including Khan al-Jumrok's facade. Since roughly 2017 (intensifying from 2019), UNESCO, the Aga Khan Trust for Culture, the Syrian Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums, and local craftsmen have carried out phased stabilization and rebuilding. Large sections of Al-Madina Souq and Jdeideh's historic houses have reopened; the Citadel's southern tower and entrance have been stabilized and the site reopened to the public; the Great Mosque's minaret reconstruction resumed formally in November 2025 and is not complete. Any claim that Aleppo's heritage is "restored" should be treated as premature — this is an ongoing, well-documented, but unfinished recovery, and conditions can change block by block. Religious sites named here — the Great Umayyad Mosque and the Forty Martyrs Cathedral — are living places of worship with active congregations, not abandoned monuments; treat them accordingly in any visitor-facing copy.

## Access Note

Syria is in a post-transition period following the fall of the Assad government in December 2024; tourism access is reopening but uneven and changes quickly. Check current government travel advisories before planning a trip rather than relying on a blanket "safe" or "unsafe" label. Aleppo International Airport resumed limited commercial flights on 24 March 2025 after a roughly three-month closure (initial routes included Syrian Air from Damascus, with Royal Jordanian test flights ahead of a wider resumption); the land route from Damascus via the M5 highway is roughly a 4–4.75 hour drive under normal conditions, though checkpoints can add time. Within the Old City, some sites (parts of the Citadel, several souq sections, the Great Mosque) are open to visitors while others remain closed, fenced, or under active restoration — expect a working construction site alongside functioning market and religious life.

## Local Flavour

Aleppo soap (sabon ghar) — a laurel-and-olive-oil bar cured for six to nine months and hand-stamped by family workshops, inscribed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in December 2024 — is sold throughout the restored souq sections. Aleppo pepper anchors the city's best-known dish, kebab halabi: minced lamb kebabs in a spiced tomato sauce, with regional variants such as cherry kebab (kebab karaz).

## Corrections Made

1. **Forty Martyrs Cathedral date (highlight entry).** The card cited "(1429)" as the cathedral's date. Wikipedia and church records show the current cathedral building was completed in **1491**, replacing an earlier chapel; 1429 is the date of a separate, older adjoining structure (the Church of the Holy Mother of God, now the Zarehian Treasury museum), not the cathedral itself. Corrected the date and clarified the two structures. Also added that the cathedral is the active seat of the Armenian Apostolic Archdiocese of Beroea, per the requirement to describe religious sites as living places of worship rather than curiosities.

2. **Baron Hotel closure year.** The card said "Closed since 2016." Multiple corroborating sources (Wikipedia, France24, Arab News, Asharq Al-Awsat) place the formal closure in **November 2014**, when the front line reached the building; the hotel continued informally sheltering refugees afterward, and government forces did not retake eastern Aleppo until December 2016 — which is likely the source of the "2016" conflation in the original card. Corrected to November 2014 and added the intervening detail.

3. **Tone: religious sites reframed as living, not decorative.** Strengthened language around the Great Umayyad Mosque and Forty Martyrs Cathedral to make explicit that both are active, in-use places of worship (the mosque holds prayers; the cathedral has an active congregation and is the seat of an archdiocese), consistent with the instruction not to present religious sites as curiosities.

4. **No changes needed, but independently re-verified against primary/near-primary sources:**
   - UNESCO inscription year (1986) and criteria (iii, iv) — confirmed via UNESCO's own listing.
   - Danger List status — confirmed still listed as of the 2025 State of Conservation report. (A Wikipedia-derived snippet surfaced during research suggested a "2013–2020" window, which does not match UNESCO's own current SOC report and appears to be a Wikidata infobox artifact rather than an actual delisting; it was discarded.)
   - 364-hectare property area — confirmed as the figure UNESCO's own nomination data uses; Wikipedia's rounded "350 ha" figure for the broader urban subdistricts is consistent within normal rounding.
   - Great Mosque founding date (715–717 CE), minaret construction (begun 1090), destruction date (24 April 2013), and the genuinely disputed responsibility for that destruction — all confirmed via Wikipedia's sourced account.
   - Minaret reconstruction resuming 30 November 2025 — confirmed by more than one search, though the underlying reporting overlapped closely; flagged as harder to independently triangulate given how recent it is.
   - Citadel earthquake damage (western Old City wall tower collapse, Ottoman mill damage, entrance/tower cracking) — confirmed via UNESCO State of Conservation reporting and contemporaneous news coverage.
   - Al-Madina Souq reopening through 2024 with several hundred shops trading — confirmed via multiple 2024–2025 reports (Project for Public Spaces, Aga Khan Development Network, and others).
   - Khan al-Wazir (1682), Khan al-Shouneh (1546), and Khan al-Jumrok's post-earthquake facade stabilization — all confirmed.
   - 2023 earthquake heritage damage estimate (US$157.8 million across 9 priority cities) — confirmed via UNESCO State of Conservation report.
   - Aleppo Ghar soap's UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription, 3 December 2024 — confirmed.
   - Aleppo International Airport's reopening on 24 March 2025 — confirmed via the U.S. Embassy in Syria and Arab News.
   - Distance from Damascus (~355–360 km, roughly 4–4.75 hours by road) — confirmed within a normal range across multiple route-distance sources; softened the drive-time estimate slightly to account for checkpoints, which the original "roughly 4 hours" did not mention.

5. **Left as appropriately hedged, no change:** the card's own `riskyClaims` — population uncertainty, the disputed cause of the minaret's 2013 destruction, the current fine-grained open/closed status of individual Citadel and souq sections, the Baron Hotel's restoration prospects, and the unverified "wrote it in Room 203" detail — were all independently checked and found to already be handled honestly (hedged, sourced as folklore, or omitted rather than asserted as fact). No changes needed there.
