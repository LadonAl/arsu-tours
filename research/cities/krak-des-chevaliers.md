# Krak des Chevaliers — قلعة الحصن

**Slug:** `krak-des-chevaliers` · **Region:** Homs Governorate, on a mountain spur overlooking the Homs Gap (the corridor linking the Mediterranean coast to inland Syria)

**One-liner:** The Hospitallers' mountain fortress that guarded Syria's Homs Gap for over two centuries under Crusader and then Mamluk control.

## Summary

Krak des Chevaliers (Arabic: *Qal'at al-Hisn*, قلعة الحصن; older name *Hisn al-Akrad*, "Fortress of the Kurds") is among the best-preserved Crusader-era castles anywhere. An earlier fortification, garrisoned by Kurdish troops under the Mirdasid emir Shibl al-Dawla Nasr, stood on the site from 1031. Between 1142 and 1144, Raymond II, Count of Tripoli, granted it to the Knights Hospitaller, who spent the following decades — building continued into the 1170s — rebuilding it into a concentric, double-walled fortress that could hold roughly 2,000 defenders at capacity, with a garrison of around 60 knights.

Perched on a hill overlooking the Homs Gap, the castle commanded the main route between the coast and inland Syria until the Mamluk sultan Baybars besieged it from 3 March to 8 April 1271 — about five weeks — and took it after the garrison surrendered, reportedly following a forged letter purporting to be from the Hospitallers' leadership authorizing surrender. British officer and scholar T.E. Lawrence, who visited in the summer of 1909 (reaching the castle around his 21st birthday) and later wrote it up as his 1910 Oxford thesis (published posthumously in 1936 as *Crusader Castles*), called it "perhaps the best preserved and most wholly admirable castle in the world."

UNESCO inscribed it as a World Heritage Site in 2006, jointly with Qal'at Salah El-Din (Saone Castle) near Latakia. During the Syrian civil war, the castle, then held by armed opposition groups, was damaged by government shelling in August 2012 (which damaged the chapel, per Wikipedia's account) and by further shelling and a Syrian Arab Air Force airstrike in July 2013, affecting towers, the Hall of the Knights and the former chapel, before government forces retook it in March 2014. UNESCO added the joint listing to the List of World Heritage in Danger in **2013** — before the recapture, not after it. The February 2023 earthquake that struck southeastern Türkiye and northern Syria caused new damage, including a partial collapse of the wall connecting the Commander's and Knights' Towers, and cracking at several other towers and the eastern wall near the chapel. Restoration by Syria's Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums (DGAM), with international technical missions (including a Syrian-Hungarian team that stabilized the chapel frescoes), is ongoing. As of a 2025 (47th session) UNESCO World Heritage Committee decision, the site remains on the Danger List, though a joint international mission visited in October 2025 to assess progress toward eventual removal. The site has reopened to visitors.

## Highlights

**Hall of the Knights (Great Hall)** — قاعة الفرسان
The Hospitallers' Gothic reception hall, with a finely carved loggia, was the castle's ceremonial centerpiece.
*Condition:* war-damaged in the 2012–2013 fighting (staircase and carved loggia elements affected). Restoration work is reported in DGAM/UNESCO materials, but this research could not confirm the specific scope of repairs to this hall (a SANA report describing vault/staircase restoration referred to the al-Zahir Baybars Tower, a different part of the castle, not confirmed here as the same work).

**Inner curtain wall and towers (incl. Commander's and Knights' Towers)** — الجدار الداخلي والأبراج
The concentric inner wall with its massive round towers is the castle's defensive core, a structure later studied as an archetype of Crusader military architecture.
*Condition:* cracked and damaged by 2012–2013 shelling and airstrikes; the wall connecting the Commander's and Knights' Towers partially collapsed in the February 2023 earthquake, with additional cracking reported at other towers and the eastern wall near the chapel — confirmed in UNESCO's 2024 State of Conservation report.

**Former chapel (later used as a mosque)** — الكنيسة/المسجد
Built as the Hospitallers' chapel; after the Mamluk conquest it was converted for use as a mosque, with two mihrabs added to the interior. Its medieval frescoes document this layered religious and civic history.
*Condition:* damaged in the 2012 shelling and 2013 fighting; frescoes stabilized and scaffolding removed by a Syrian-Hungarian conservation mission in recent restoration campaigns.

**Outer defensive wall and moat**
A rock-cut moat and outer wall studded with towers form the castle's second line of defense, part of the concentric design that helped it resist sieges for over a century.
*Condition:* largely intact; sections of the stable and counterscarp walls have undergone restoration and reconstruction in recent years.

**Inner courtyard cistern**
A vaulted water cistern in the inner courtyard helped sustain the garrison during sieges.
*Condition:* vault restored as part of post-war conservation work.

*(Note: the Arabic labels above for individual interior features are descriptive translations, not verified as official historical or architectural names for each space — only the castle's own name, "Qal'at al-Hisn" / قلعة الحصن, was independently confirmed as an attested Arabic name.)*

## UNESCO Status

Inscribed 2006 as "Crac des Chevaliers and Qal'at Salah El-Din" (joint listing). Added to the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013. As of the World Heritage Committee's most recent published decision (47 COM 7A.52, 2025 — 47th session), the property **remains** on the Danger List. A joint UNESCO international mission visited the site in October 2025 to assess corrective measures, with participants expressing hope this could support eventual removal from the Danger List — but as of this research (September 2026) it has not been removed.

*Correction to original brief: an earlier note in this research referenced a "1979" inscription date. That figure could not be corroborated by UNESCO's own records and is incorrect; 2006 is the verified inscription year.*

## Conservation Note

The castle took direct war damage during the civil war: government shelling in August 2012 damaged the chapel, and further shelling and a July 2013 Syrian Arab Air Force airstrike (amid fighting for nearby Homs) damaged towers, the Hall of the Knights' staircase and carved elements, and the former chapel. Government forces recaptured the site in March 2014. Since then, Syria's Directorate-General of Antiquities and Museums — still active under the post-2024 transitional government as of 2025 — has worked with UNESCO and international technical missions on emergency consolidation and phased restoration, including wall reconstruction reported in DGAM/UNESCO materials and fresco stabilization by a Syrian-Hungarian mission. The February 2023 earthquake added new damage: a partial collapse of the wall connecting the Commander's and Knights' Towers, and new/widened cracking at several other towers and the eastern wall. As of the 2024 State of Conservation report and the 2025 (47th session) Committee decision, the property remains on the Danger List; restoration is ongoing and incremental, not complete.

## Getting There

From Damascus, the general route runs north on the M1 highway toward Homs (roughly 160 km), then west from Homs toward the village of Al-Husn, where the castle sits (commonly cited as roughly 40 km further). Total distance is commonly given as approximately 200 km, but **quoted travel times vary significantly across sources** — from under two hours to 3–4 hours for the full journey — likely reflecting differences in road conditions, checkpoints, and route choice rather than a single reliable figure. Travelers should confirm current timing with a local operator rather than relying on a fixed estimate.

## Best Season

Spring (March–May) and autumn (September–November) for milder mountain weather; the site is exposed and hot in summer, and cold and windy in winter.

## Access Note

The castle has reopened to visitors, and multiple 2025 sources describe active tourism, including tour operators running trips since spring 2025. **Current (September 2026) official travel-advisory status, permit requirements, and opening hours/ticket prices were not independently verified in this research pass** — check a current foreign-ministry advisory and local tour operators before treating this as authoritative access information. Access varies by route, security conditions, and administration; confirm the specific advisory in force at the time of travel.

## Local Flavour

The nearby town of Al-Husn and the wider Homs Gap countryside sit near Wadi al-Nasara ("the Valley of the Christians"), a region long home to a mix of Christian and Muslim mountain villages with their own culinary traditions — look for local olive oil, regional kibbeh varieties, and mountain-grown produce sold at small roadside markets near the castle approach.
