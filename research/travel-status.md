# Syria Travel Status — Research Notes

**Compiled:** 21 September 2026
**Purpose:** Practical, sourced snapshot of the current state of travel to Syria for planning purposes. Syria is in a post-transition period following the fall of the Assad government in December 2024. This is a working research document, not public-facing copy — figures and statuses should be re-verified close to publication, since several of them (airline schedules, card payments, currency swap) are changing month to month.

**Tone note for downstream use:** Nothing below should be compressed into a blanket "safe" or "unsafe" claim. Advisory levels and reasons are quoted from the issuing government; where sources vary by region, that variation is preserved rather than flattened.

## 2. Visa policy (as of 2026)

- **Visa on arrival is the norm for most nationalities.** Syria operates a reciprocity-based visa-on-arrival system at both airports and land borders for the majority of visitors — the cost of the visa scales with how difficult/expensive it is for Syrian citizens to obtain a visa to the visitor's home country.
- **Pre-approval required for some nationalities.** Citizens of a specific list of countries (reported to include Afghanistan, Belarus, China, India, Indonesia, Iraq, Lebanon, Pakistan, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Yemen, and African countries generally) must obtain pre-approval, reportedly taking 3–4 weeks, rather than receiving a visa on arrival.
- **E-visa status is unclear/conflicting between sources.** The UK FCDO's entry-requirements page still directs applicants to an electronic visa portal (evisa.sy) for visa applications and status checks. Separately, a travel-guide source (In Your Pocket) states the official e-visa site evisa.sy is "currently inactive" and that Syrian embassies do not issue tourist visas, with visa-on-arrival being the only route. **This is a direct contradiction between sources and needs to be re-verified against the Syrian Ministry of Foreign Affairs or Ministry of Interior directly before publishing anything definitive** — it could reflect the e-visa system being intermittently down rather than discontinued.
- **No tour-operator sponsorship or letter-of-invitation requirement was found** in any source reviewed (FCDO, In Your Pocket, Canadian government) for ordinary tourist entry via visa-on-arrival. Canada's advisory does flag that non-Arab nationals cannot get visas at the border and must secure them from Syrian diplomatic missions in advance — this appears to conflict somewhat with "visa on arrival for most visitors" and should be treated as nationality-dependent rather than resolved here.
- **Visa duration:** airport arrivals typically receive a 15-day single-entry visa; land-border arrivals typically receive 15- or 30-day single-entry visas (per travel-guide source, not a primary government citation — re-verify).
- **Fees:** reported range from free (e.g. Jordan, Lebanon, Malaysia, Mauritania, Serbia) up to $300 for a small number of countries; most nationalities fall in a $50–$150 range. Payment in cash USD is preferred.
- **Entry bans:** Israeli and Iranian nationals are not permitted entry; passport stamps evidencing a visit to Israel result in refusal.
- **Registration:** Canada's advisory notes stays exceeding 14 days require registration with the Syrian Immigration and Passports Office by day 15, and passports must be valid 6+ months beyond the planned departure date.

## 3. Airports and airlines

### Damascus International Airport (DAM)
- Physically open and handling scheduled traffic through 2026, but service has been **repeatedly disrupted by regional escalations**, not steadily normal.
- Carriers reported operating to Damascus at various points in 2026: Syrian Airlines (Syrian Air), Cham Wings, Qatar Airways (resumed daily service 1 May 2026), Kuwait Airways (resumed 15 July 2026 after a 14-year gap), Turkish Airlines, flydubai, Royal Jordanian, Air Arabia, flynas, Jazeera Airways, AJet, Etihad, and a KLM Amsterdam–Damascus route that resumed 2 July 2026 after 14 years.
- **June 2026 disruption:** after a US–Iran ceasefire reportedly collapsed and Iran resumed strikes across the Gulf in early June 2026, several airlines that had restarted Damascus service in April–May 2026 re-suspended it, and EASA (EU aviation safety agency) advised operators against flying in the Damascus Flight Information Region (FIR) at any altitude. This is the most serious caveat found: Syrian airspace risk status has fluctuated with the wider Iran-related regional conflict, not just with internal Syrian security.
- No source in this pass gives a clean, dated September 2026 snapshot of exactly which airlines are flying that week — this needs a direct check against an airline schedule or FlightAware (https://www.flightaware.com/live/airport/OSDI) close to any travel date.

### Aleppo International Airport (ALP)
- Resumed scheduled flights 20 January 2026 after a security-related suspension.
- 5 March 2026: Syria's civil aviation authority reactivated further air corridors; Syrian Airlines flew Aleppo–Istanbul, with Aleppo–Jeddah following.
- By July 2026: Aleppo was handling scheduled service including Sharjah (Air Arabia, from 4 July), and earlier Jazeera Airways and Pegasus services (June 2026), plus Kuwait and Istanbul routes.

**Caveat:** all of the above reflects a pattern of progressive resumption interrupted by security-driven suspensions — treat "which airlines fly there" as a moving target requiring a recheck within days of any actual booking, not a fixed fact.

## 5. Currency and payments

- **Redenomination completed in 2026.** The Central Bank of Syria cut two zeros from the pound: 100 old Syrian pounds = 1 new Syrian pound. New banknotes entered circulation 1 January 2026 (public circulation from 3 January 2026). This is a technical redenomination — it does not change real purchasing power, only removes large numerals from daily transactions.
- **Swap progress:** by 4 May 2026, 56% of old notes had been replaced; by 4 June, 63%; by 30 July 2026, 95%, effectively ending the changeover.
- **Design:** new notes drop political imagery in favor of shared cultural/agricultural symbols — part of the transitional government's institutional rebranding.
- **Context:** the pound is reported to have lost over 99% of its value over the preceding decade due to conflict, capital flight, sanctions, and contracted domestic production.
- **Cash-only reality is easing but not resolved.** As of the most recent sources (August 2026), Syria has historically been a cash-only environment for visitors with no reliable ATM access. This is actively changing:
  - Late August 2026: Visa and Mastercard each completed first international card transactions in Syria. Mastercard, via QNB, enabled participating Syrian hotels, restaurants, and government entities to accept international Mastercard payments at point-of-sale terminals.
  - Visa's rollout began with a December 2025 agreement with Syria's central bank; a symbolic transaction by Syria's president at a Damascus restaurant was reported in August 2026.
  - Plans reported to deploy 50,000 point-of-sale devices by end of 2026.
  - Despite this, current guidance for visitors (as of the most recent sources) is still to **bring sufficient cash (USD preferred, crisp/new bills) and not rely on ATMs**, since card acceptance is limited to a rolling set of participating hotels/restaurants, not general use.

## 6. Sanctions status (post-December 2024 through 2026)

This is the area with the most consequential change since the transition, and it is directly tied to the currency/payments picture above.

**United States:**
- 30 June 2025: President Trump issued Executive Order 14312, removing US sanctions on Syria, effective 1 July 2025.
- 18 December 2025: the Caesar Syria Civilian Protection Act of 2019 (Caesar Act) was repealed via the FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act.
- 24 August 2026: Syria's State Sponsor of Terrorism designation was rescinded.
- 26 August 2026: OFAC removed the Syrian Sanctions Regulations from the Code of Federal Regulations.
- **Targeted sanctions remain**: on Bashar al-Assad and his associates, human-rights abusers, Captagon traffickers, individuals tied to past weapons-proliferation activity, and ISIS/al-Qa'ida and Iran-aligned actors. This means "comprehensive" sanctions are gone but a targeted/individual-designation regime persists — relevant for any due diligence on business counterparts.

**European Union:**
- 24 February 2025: EU suspended (not yet lifted) certain sectoral sanctions — banking, energy, transport among them — to support the transition.
- 28 May 2025: EU formally lifted most economic sanctions on Syria, including those suspended in February, covering banking/finance, energy, transport, and other sectors.
- EU guidance (November 2025 FAQ) confirms no EU-side restriction on using SWIFT or other financial messaging systems for Syria.

**Banking practicality:** the US reportedly allowed Syrian banks back into SWIFT and correspondent banking relationships by mid-2025. Combined with the EU banking-sector delisting, this is what has made the Visa/Mastercard rollout described in Section 5 possible.

**What this means practically for tourism businesses:** international payment processing, hotel/booking-platform integration, and correspondent banking are now legally permissible under both US and EU sanctions regimes (subject to the remaining targeted designations), which was not true before mid-2025. This is a genuine, dateable inflection point — not yet matched by full on-the-ground payment infrastructure (see Section 5's caveat on rolling, partial card acceptance).

## 7. Connectivity: SIM cards and internet

- **Foreign visitors can now get a local SIM.** As of 2026 sources, visitors can obtain a Syriatel or MTN prepaid line or eSIM at official branches after passport registration (entry stamp required). Airport kiosks for both operators have historically existed at Damascus International, though hours are reported as irregular.
- **Requirements:** passport with Syria entry stamp, an unlocked phone, and a written local address (hotel/apartment).
- **Cost:** a new local line reported at roughly 500 Syrian pounds (new currency) ≈ $4 USD.
- **Coverage:** workable for messaging, maps, and occasional video calls in Damascus, Aleppo, Latakia, and Homs; rural and conflict-affected areas can drop to 2G or no signal.
- **VPN:** described as "essential" for unblocking certain apps/sites — implies ongoing restrictions or connectivity gaps rather than open, unrestricted internet.
- **International eSIM providers:** as of an April 2026 source, Airalo does not offer a Syria eSIM plan — Syria remains outside coverage for several major global eSIM providers, though at least one smaller provider (LinkeSIM) was reported to cover it.

What can be said with reasonable confidence from the sources gathered:

## Summary for the requester

Syria's transition-era travel picture as of 21 September 2026 is one of **rapid, real change running ahead of formal advisory status**: land borders with Lebanon and Jordan are busy and being upgraded, Damascus and Aleppo airports have brought back double-digit numbers of airlines since December 2024, sanctions that blocked banking and card payments have been substantially removed by both the US and EU through 2025–2026, and the currency has been redenominated. At the same time, **every Western government advisory checked (US, UK, Germany, Australia, Canada) still places Syria at its highest formal risk category**, none has downgraded it, airspace and border status have shown they can revert quickly around regional military escalation (the June 2026 Iran-related disruption to Damascus flights, the April 2026 Masnaa closure), and practical infrastructure — especially card payments and reliable connectivity — is only partially rolled out. Visa policy, e-visa availability, and several regional-access questions came back with conflicting or incomplete sourcing this pass and need direct follow-up before being stated as settled fact in any client-facing material.
